The scientific name of mountain rush is _Juncus arcticus_ Willd. subsp. _littoralis_(Willd.) Trautv. (Juncaceae). It is a variety of arctic rush (_Juncus arcticus_ Willd.). There is 1 other subspecies of arctic rush [197]:

 * _Juncus arcticus_ Willd. subsp. _alaskanus_ (Hulten) S. L. Welsh, Alaskan rush

Mountain rush occurs from sea level to subalpine zones in montane habitats [74,90,115] as well as in wet areas of Southwestern deserts [74,102] in wet meadows, along streambanks, rivers, lakes, ponds, bogs, sloughs, freshwater and brackish marshes and sometimes in dry flats and meadows [21,54,83,88,125,141,142,204,208,211]. It is common around coastal areas and near the Great Lakes and other major watercourses [45,117,126,139,180,197]. It is characteristic on coastal, low montane, and northern Great Plains sites [74,90,115], and alpine zones [90].

### Soils

Mountain rush generally grows on poorly-drained sandy to silty loam soils, often with a thick organic layer [32,76,102,126,139,179]. It is adapted to coarse, medium, and fine textured soils [197]. Soil orders and suborders on which mountain rush grows included Mollisols, Entisols, Borolls, Cryaquolls, Haploxerolls, Fluvaquentic Haploborolls, and aquic or calcic Cryoborolls [25,79,120]. Mountain rush is found in sites where the water level is from 4 inches (10 cm) above to 40 inches (100 cm) below the soil surface [25,144,186,221]. It is found on sandy soils in the sand barrens of northwestern Wisconsin and shorelines of ponds and lakes in northwestern Alberta [126,139].

Mountain rush is well adapted to alkaline soils and environments [19,24,26,59,71,74,102,106,208]. Mountain rush has been found growing on highly alkaline soils with a pH greater than 8.4 in Glacier County, Montana [151]. In the northern prairie potholes region of the United States and Canada, mountain rush can withstand salinity levels measured as specific conductivity (mS) between 0.1 and 20.1 in temporarily, seasonally, and semipermanently flooded and saturated water or moisture regimes [100]. In the peatlands at Candle Lake, Saskatchewan, growth was most successful in soils with a pH level of 5.0-5.9. It was found to a lower extent in pH soils of 6.0 to 6.9 and 7.0 to 7.9, respectively [98].

Mountain rush grows on soils with a pH level ranging from 7.8 to 9.1 in a western Utah salt marsh. Maximum, median, and minimum soil pH levels at 3 depths in rush meadow and rush border communities where mountain rush is the dominant species are presented below [19]:

### Climate

Mountain rush grows in a wide range of precipitation and temperature zones and requires a minimum of 90 frost free days for growth [197]. Average yearly precipitation extremes where mountain rush communities are found range from 7.13 inches (181 mm) in Juab County, Utah [19] to 45 inches (1,140 mm) in the Wasatch Mountains of Utah [99]. Temperature extremes range from over 120 °F (49 °C) at Afton Canyon in the Mojave Desert, California [119], to –50 °F (–46 °C) at Yellowstone National Park, Wyoming [73].

## Plant Communities

Mountain rush occurs in numerous habitat and community types. In mixed and deciduous forests it is often found with willow (_Salix_ spp.) and pine (_Pinus_ spp.) species. In grassland and streambank communities common associates include Kentucky bluegrass (_Poa pratensis_) and sedge (_Carex_ spp.) species. On alkaline soils mountain rush often occurs with saltgrass (_Distichlis spicata_).

### Northwest

Mountain rush occurs in deciduous and mixed forests, salt marshes, shrub- and grassland, streambank, and tundra communities.

**Deciduous and Mixed Forests**

Mountain rush is a dominant species with Kentucky bluegrass on subirrigated sandy rangeland along the shoreline of Beaverhill Lake, approximately 50 miles (80 km) east of Edmonton. Mountain rush occurs at a frequency of 35%. As lake levels have receded the past 60 years exposing new soil, the mountain rush-Kentucky bluegrass association colonized the uplands but was invaded by balsam popular (_Populus balsamifera_ ssp. _balsamifera_), quaking aspen (_P. tremuloides_), Bebb willow (_S. bebbiana_), pussy willow (_S. discolor_), meadow willow (_S. petiolaris_), and fall willow (_S. serissima_). Common understory forbs and grasses included Virginia strawberry (_Fragaria virginiana_), manyflowered aster (_Symphyotrichum ericoides_ var. _pansum_), and slender wheatgrass (_Elymus trachycaulus_) [8].

**Salt Marsh Communities**

A mountain rush-Pacific silverweed-tufted hairgrass (_Argentina egedii_ ssp. _egedii_ \- _Deschampsia cespitosa_) union is described for the Salmon River estuary in northwest Oregon [67]. The mountain rush-clustered field sedge-balsam groundsel (_Carex praegracilis_ -_Packera paupercula_) association is common in Canadian prairie province salt marshes and meadows and mountain rush is found in transition zones from salt to freshwater in the common river grass (_Scolochloa festucacea_) association [117,118]. Mountain rush occurs as a dominant species at Sand Lake, an estuary on the Oregon Coast 12 miles (19 km) south of Tillamook [194]. On the west coast of Vancouver Island, British Columbia, mountain rush is a dominant species with Pacific silverweed [149].

**Shrub- and Grassland Communities**

Mountain rush's presence in Kentucky bluegrass communities is noted by numerous authors [8,18,23,35,36,61,106,117,130,131,178,201,202,203,210]. Mountain rush occurs as a dominant species in late-spring and early-summer flooded-meadows at the Malheur National Wildlife Refuge, Oregon. Other dominant grass, sedge, rush (_Juncus_ spp.), and forb species in the area include redtop (_Agrostis gigantea_), prairie cordgrass (_Spartina pectinata_), meadow barley (_Hordeum brachyantherum_ ssp. _brachyantherum_), timothy (_Phleum pratense_), slenderbeak sedge (_C. athrostachya_), needleleaf sedge (_C. duriuscula_), Sierra rush (_J. nevadensis_), western yarrow (_Achillea millefolium_), horse cinquefoil (_Potentilla hippiana_), and chaffweed (_Anagallis minima_) [42]. Mountain rush is common in sedge-dominated wet mountain meadows of Wyoming [43] and in the beaked sedge (_C. rostrata_) community type in Montana where it has an average canopy cover of 31% and a constancy of 24% [78]. Mountain rush is characteristic on the northern Alberta range and cultivated grasslands as is plains rough fescue (_Festuca hallii_), rough fescue (_F. altaica_), and shortbristle needle-and-thread (_Hesperostipa curtiseta_) [89]. In the Slave River Lowlands, Northwest Territories, mountain rush is a dominant species on dry meadows with a mean occurrence of 44% [150]. Mountain rush grows on rough fescue-Parry's oatgrass (_Danthonia parryi_) dominated grasslands in Banff and Jasper national parks, Alberta [185]. Mountain rush occupies sites in southern Wyoming and southwestern Montana where mountain big sagebrush (_Artemisia tridentata_ ssp. _vaseyana_) and Wyoming big sagebrush (_A. t._ ssp. _wyomingensis_) are the dominant species [189,206]. In Glacier National Park, Montana, mountain rush has a 1% canopy cover value in timothy-dominated grasslands [196]. A mountain rush emergent community occurs as a narrow band between alkaline or upland communities and more mesic tufted hairgrass communities at the Chilly Slough Wetland Conservation Area in central Idaho [217].

**Streambank Communities**

Mountain rush is one of several dominant plant species along streambanks in Harney County, Oregon. Mountain rush is interspersed with Nebraska sedge (_C. nebrascensis_), wooly sedge (_C. lanuginosa_), Kentucky bluegrass, and redtop [23]. Plant species listed above are found at Wet Creek in east-central Idaho as well, as are beaked sedge, water sedge (_C. aquatilis_), smallwing sedge (_C. microptera_), western mountain aster (_Symphyotrichum spathulatum_ var. _spathulatum_), Rocky Mountain iris (_Iris missouriensis_), and longleaf phlox (_Phlox longifolia_). The most prolific woody plants at Wet Creek include Booth's willow (_Salix boothii_), Geyer willow (_S. geyeriana_), shrubby cinquefoil (_Dasiphora floribunda_), Wood's rose (_Rosa woodsii_), currant (_Ribes_ spp.), and water birch (_Betula occidentalis_) [36]. The mountain rush-Douglas' sedge (_C. douglasii_) association is common along the banks of Mann Creek in west-central Idaho [122]. Mountain rush is a dominant rush species with an average cover value of 63.8% and frequency value of 100% along Silver Bow Creek in western Montana [170].

**Tundra Communities**

Mountain rush occurs in the Yukon Territory in borrow pits. The disturbed sites were created by the extraction of fill for construction of the Dempster Highway and the Canadian Oil (CANOL) Pipeline and road system. The mean cover value for mountain rush at the Dempster and CANOL sites are 4.11% and 17.37%, respectively [105].

### Southwest

Mountain rush is found in coniferous, deciduous, and mixed forests, shrub- and grassland, salt marsh, and streambank communities.

**Coniferous Forests**

Mountain rush is sometimes present in patches or scattered clumps in interior ponderosa pine (_Pinus ponderosa_ var. _scopulorum_) ranges in Colorado [44]. Mountain rush is found in wet and dry meadows invaded by Sierra lodgepole pine (_P. contorta_ var. _murrayana_) in Yosemite National Park, California. Cover value of mountain rush in Sierra lodgepole pine stands ranges from 0.5% to 5.0% [86]. Mountain rush is a dominant species in meadows surrounded by shore pine (_P. c._ var. _contorta_), Pacific ponderosa pine (_P. p._ var. _ponderosa_), Jeffrey pine (_P. jeffreyi_), coast Douglas fir (_Pseudotsuga menziesii_ var. _menziesii_), and white fir (_Abies concolor_) mixed forests located near Big Grizzly Creek, northern California [94]. It occurs in Great Basin bristlecone pine (_Pinus longaeva_) forests in the White Mountains of California between 9,500 feet (2,900 m) and 11,000 feet (3,400 m) [216]

**Deciduous and Mixed Forests**

Mountain rush is an understory species in New Mexico Fremont cottonwood-Goodding willow (_Populus fremontii-S. gooddingii_) forests along the Rio Grande River [30]. The sandbar willow (_S. exigua_)-mountain rush community type occurs along stretches of the Rio Grande River [60]. Mountain rush is a dominant species in Arizona Fremont cottonwood-Goodding willow forests along the San Pedro River [187]. In central New Mexico, Mountain rush occurs along and near the Pecos River in Colorado pinyon-oneseed juniper (_Pinus edulis-Juniperus monosperma_) and interior ponderosa pine-Gambel oak (_Quercus gambelii_) woodlands [32].

**Shrub- and Grassland Communities**

Mountain rush's presence in Kentucky bluegrass communities is noted by several authors [32,33,35,62,92,128,137,146,154]. Mountain rush is common along spring-fed cattle trough drainageways in mountain big sagebrush and low sagebrush (_Artemisia arbuscula_) communities in northeastern Nevada [14]. Mountain rush's presence in mountain sagebrush communities in the Sierra Nevada of California and the Wasatch Mountains of Utah is also noted [41,82,218]. In Arizona's Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument, Mountain rush grows around springs with beaked spikerush (_Eleocharis rostellata_), Olney threesquare (_Scirpus_ _americanus_), curlycup gumweed (_Grindelia squarrosa_), Parish's desert-thorn (_Lycium parishii_), and lotebush (_Ziziphus obtusifolia_) [15,148]. Mountain rush is common in sedge-dominated mountain wet meadows of Colorado [43].

Ciénegas are unique freshwater wetlands found in semiarid grasslands of Arizona. Mountain rush occurs on dry soils on the periphery of ciénegas which lack canopy cover and are dominated by deergrass (_Muhlenbergia rigens_) and great ragweed (_Ambrosia trifida_) [46]. Mountain rush is a dominant species on ancient lake sediments in the Ruby Valley, Nevada [133]. Mountain rush is a dominant species with 86.6% frequency and 16.3% coverage in dry meadows near North Cat Creek, western Nevada [169]. Mountain rush is found on Rocky Mountain iris-dominated meadows on Mount Pinos, California [200]. In Nevada, mountain rush occurs on alkali flats and wet alkaline soil with saltbush (_Atriplex_ spp.), blackbrush (_Coleogyne ramosissima_), and sagebrush (_Artemisia_ spp.), and in pinyon-juniper (_Juniperus_ spp.) and California red fir (_Abies magnifica_) communities [102].

**Salt Marsh Communities**

Mountain rush is a dominant species in rush meadow and rush border communities within spring-fed salt marshes in Juab County, Utah. Rush border communities are described as strips of vegetation (from a few inches to several feet) bordering the winding shorelines of numerous sloughs in the study area. In rush meadow and border communities, mountain rush frequency is 100% and average density/ft² is 23.2 and 37.2, respectively. Common associated species in rush meadow and border communities include saltgrass, alkali sacaton (_Sporobolus airoides_), showy milkweed (_Asclepias speciosa_), and common reed (_Phragmites australis_). Further species found in rush meadows include Utah swampfire (_Sarcocornia utahensis_), iodinebush (_Allenrolfea occidentalis_), and fiddleleaf hawksbeard (_Crepis runcinata_). Mountain rush occurs exclusively in the saltgrass-alkali sacaton community with a frequency of 20% and an average density/ft² of 1.8 [19]. Mountain rush (16% cover) is a dominant species in the mixed grass-sedge-rush meadows surrounding Utah Lake, Utah [26,27,28]. Mountain rush (49.0% mean cover value) is dominant in salt meadows of Goshen Bay in Utah County, Utah [176].

**Streambank Communities**

Mountain rush is an important understory species in planeleaf (_Salix planifolia_), Geyer, peachleaf (_S. amygdaloides_), coyote (_S. exigua_ ssp. _exigua_ var. _exigua_), and yellow willow (S. lutea) communities along Sheep Creek in northern Larimer County, CO [92]. Mountain rush is a dominant species in Afton Canyon located in the lower Mojave River drainage of the western Mojave Desert, California [119]. Mountain rush is common in southern cattail (_Typha domingensis_)-common reed-dominated wet meadows along the Colorado River, Arizona [183].

### North-central/South-central

Mountain rush is found in grassland, lakeshore, and salt marsh communities.

**Grassland Communities**

Mountain rush is found in the Nebraska sandhills [126]. Mountain rush is a common associate in sedge meadows in the upper Midwest [157]. Mountain rush is a common associated species on red fescue (_F. rubra_) dominated meadows on the Hudson Bay lowlands of northern Manitoba [162]. Mountain rush also occurs in the Nebraska sandhills which are dominated by prairie sandreed (_Calamovilfa longifolia_), sand bluestem (_Andropogon gerardii_ var. _paucipilus_), little bluestem (_Schizachyrium scoparium_), and switchgrass (_Panicum virgatum_) [171,195]. Mountain rush is found in swales with and without western prairie fringed orchid (_Platanhera praeclara_) in the Sheyenne National Grassland, North Dakota [17]. In swales with orchids, Mountain rush's average canopy cover is 5.4% and 1.7% in swales without orchids [212]. Mountain rush is commonly a dominant species on hayed, temporarily flooded plains and prairies in the northern prairie potholes region of the United States and Canada [100]. Mountain rush is widespread and characteristic in wetter, poorly-drained areas of the tallgrass prairies of the United States and Canada, a community type that stretches from southern Manitoba to south-central and eastern Texas where it contacts the coastal prairie [109,177].

**Lakeshore Communities**

Mountain rush is described at the most characteristic plant surrounding pool margins on the upper beach of Lake Michigan [45]. It is a dominant species on the broad sand beaches of lakes in northwestern Wisconsin, an area known as the "sand barrens" [126]. Mountain rush occurs with an average frequency of 1% and a constancy of 10% in little bluestem dominated grasslands at Point Beach State Forest, Two Rivers, Wisconsin [198].

**Salt Marsh Communities**

The Mountain rush-clustered field sedge-balsam groundsel association is common in Canadian prairie province salt marshes and meadows, and mountain rush is found in transition zones from salt to freshwater in the common river grass association [117,118].

### Northeast

Mountain rush is found in lakeshore, pond, and salt marsh communities.

**Lakeshore and Pond Communities**

Mountain rush, chaffy sedge (_Carex paleacea_), marsh straw sedge (_C. hormathodes_), and prairie cordgrass are dominant species along the Saint Lawrence River, southern Quebec [163]. Mountain rush is indicative of wet meadows in the Mendon Ponds Park, New York [173]

**Salt Marsh Communities**

Mountain rush is a dominant species in willow thickets in salt marshes of James Bay, Ontario [70].

## Botanical and Ecological Characteristics

## Botanical Description

![A photo of a round rush stem with an inflorescence emerging from partway up the stem with clusters of small, intricate flowers.](https://research.fs.usda.gov/sites/default/files/styles/large/public/2025-11/feis-junarcl_flowers.jpg?itok=SMqZmfs8)

Photo Credit

Photo by Peri Lee Pipkin, iNaturalist.org, some rights reserved. 

Flowering mountain rush in Bernalillo County, New Mexico ([CC BY-NC 4.0](<https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nc/4.0/>)).

This description provides characteristics that may be relevant to fire ecology, and is not meant for identification. Keys for identification are available [2,21,53,55,71,74,83,88,115,125,141,180,188,208,211]

Mountain rush is a sod-forming, cool season, perennial graminoid [48,53,83,88,153,156,166,188]. At maturity, mountain rush is 1.1 to 3.6 feet (0.4-1.1 m) tall and grows in small clusters [125,211]. The average life span of a mountain rush plant is between 8 and 10 years [181]. Mountain rush has a low tolerance to drought [197]. The culms of mountain rush are leafless, erect, terete, stout (1-6 mm thick), 14 to 43 inches (35-110 cm) tall, and arise from creeping rhizomes [76,188]. With the exception of the involucre, mountain rush is comprised solely of rounded, clustered basal leaves approximately 0.8 to 6 inches (2-15 cm) long [2,76,141,188,211]. Mountain rush produces 5 to 50 or more perfect flowers with the perianth segments 3 to 6 mm long [113,115,180,211]. It produces about 2 to 25 flowers [180]. Seeds of mountain rush are striate capsules 0.4 to 1 mm tall and 0.4 mm wide [48,136,180,188].

The rhizomes of mountain rush are usually found in shallow soil, but the root depth varies greatly. A study conducted in the Sheldon Antelope Refuge, Nevada, found that mountain rush rhizomes never reached a depth greater than 4 inches (10 cm) but that large roots (>0.9 mm in diameter) and fine roots (<0.9 mm in diameter) often reach depths greater than 16 inches (40 cm) [121]. One study reports roots reaching depths of 20 inches (60 cm) [181]. Mountain rush has stringy, massive, slightly branched roots measuring 0.5 to 2.5 mm in diameter and not always broadest at the base. On many of the active roots a zone of hairs develops, which facilitate the intake of moisture [181]

Mountain rush with flowering heads collected in the Boise and Sawtooth national forests, Idaho, were cut and measured in July. Mountain rush regenerates via seeds and rhizomes [181,188]. Mountain rush primarily regenerates via rhizomes though each year it flowers and fruits intensively. Generally, only the "youngest" parts of the rhizomes produce a variable number of shoots [181].

### Breeding System

Mountain rush is monoecious [95,188].

### Pollination

Mountain rush is wind pollinated [159]. The pollination of mountain rush by bumble bees has been observed in a salt marsh on Vancouver Island, British Columbia [149].

### Seed Production

Mountain rush fruits "intensively" each year. Shoots of mountain rush generally must grow on a 3 to 4 year old rhizome before they produce fruiting flowers [181]. The number of mountain rush seeds produced per inflorescence varies considerably among populations [95].

### Seed Dispersal

Wind is a primary disperser of mountain rush seeds. In a European dune environment almost 50% of mountain rush seeds were carried away by wind [181].

### Seed Banking

Mountain rush has a persistent seed bank [150]. Mountain rush seed banks can survive fire (See Immediate Fire Effects) [34,207]. Mountain rush seed banks (mean number of seeds/m² ± s x)in 2 locations (Morgan Canyon and Bob Scott) within the central Nevada Humboldt-Toiyabe National Forest were 1,192±230 and 100±56, respectively [207].

### Germination

Moisture is necessary for mountain rush seed germination. In sandy environments with a scarcity of moisture the germination rate of mountain rush seeds was found to be 0%. Mountain rush seeds collected by the United States Department of Agriculture and used to rehabilitate riparian and wetland disturbances had a viability rate between 28% to 96% and did not decline in viability after 17 months of storage in closed glass containers at room temperature [174]

### Seedling Establishment and Plant Growth

Mountain rush seedling vigor is classified as medium [197]. Once seedling establishment begins, mountain rush takes 2 to 3 years to attain maturity and is still incapable of flowering. Flowering generally does not occur in plants younger than 4 years. Mountain rush seedlings observed after 1 month of growth developed a hair-shaped cotyledon and 3 to 5 grass-like leaves 0.6 to 1.4 inch (1.5-3.5 cm) in length and 0.5 to 1.0 mm wide [181,182].

### Vegetative Regeneration

Mountain rush reproduces asexually from rhizomes. Generally, only the "youngest" parts of the rhizomes produce a variable number of shoots [181].

## Successional Status

Mountain rush is shade intolerant [181,197] and is found in all stages of succession [19,78,96,105,117,126,203]. Mountain rush is found on "primary succession" sites in the Yukon Territory. The sites are borrow pits created by the extraction of fill for the construction of the Dempster Highway and the Canadian Oil Pipeline and road system [105]. Mountain rush is described as a late seral to "climax" species in the beaked sedge wetlands habitat-type distributed throughout Montana [78]. Mountain rush dominated rush meadow and border communities in western Utah salt marshes are described as permanent communities [19]. It is sometimes described as a mid-seral species, giving way to shrubby or woody vegetation in Canadian prairie province salt marshes and meadows [117]. Mountain rush occurs as a late-seral species in dry bog communities in the sand barrens of northwestern Wisconsin [126].

Mountain rush, Kentucky bluegrass, and/or Nebraska sedge may replace wooly sedge in areas of severe disturbance such as heavily grazed meadows susceptible to trampling and soil compaction. Mountain rush and/or Kentucky bluegrass may replace Nebraska sedge when it is subjected to heavy grazing and the water table is lowered [96]. Mountain rush increases substantially on poor condition wet meadow sites in the central Oregon pumice zone [203].

## Fire Ecology and Management

## Immediate Fire Effects

Mountain rush is top-killed by fire, with rhizomes and seeds protected by insulating soil [34,207,220].

Mountain rush seed banks survive fire, but the number of seeds is less in burned areas compared to unburned areas [34,207]. Within the tufted hairgrass-sedge community-type seed bank of Yellowstone National Park, mountain rush seeds occurred in the seed bank in densities of 1,667 seeds/m² on unburned sites and 493 seeds/m² on sites burned during the Yellowstone fire of 1988 [34]. ## Fire Adaptations

Mountain rush is fire tolerant when dormant [197] and top-killed by fire during the growing season [34,207,220]. It establishes after fire through seed and/or lateral spread by rhizomes. Mountain rush fruits lack an awn; therefore, initial seed dispersal onto burned sites is primarily effected by wind.

## Plant Response to Fire

Mountain rush has a high tolerance to fire [197]. Mountain rush recovers from fire by rhizomatous spread and/or establishing by seed. Fire has a positive effect on mountain rush frequency and coverage [8,82,150,206,214,215,220]. Some research suggests that postfire mountain rush plants have a faster growth rate and attain a greater mature height than plants growing on unburned sites [220].

Prescribed fire was used to control an invasive mixed poplar-willow forest on mountain rush-Kentucky bluegrass grasslands in central Alberta along the shore of Beaverhill Lake. Burning occurred on 18 May 1971, on four 0.62-acre (0.25 ha) plots. Five years after the controlled burn, mountain rush canopy cover was significantly (p<0.05) higher on burned plots (60%) than unburned plots (35%) [8].

In the fall of 1981, a 64-acre (26-ha) plot of Great Basin meadow at Inyo National Forest, California, underwent a prescribed burn to restore brush-encroached meadows. mountain rush's average coverage significantly (p<0.01) increased on burned dry meadow plots and increased as well on burned wet meadow plots but not significantly [82]:

## Fire Regimes

Many diverse communities provide mountain rush habitat. Mountain rush experiences extreme ranges in fire frequency. In the northern and southern cordgrass prairies where mountain rush is found fire may occur every 1 to 3 years [145]. Conversely, mountain rush also occurs in sugar maple communities where the fire return interval is 1,000 years or more [205].

Mountain rush is a dominant species in Afton Canyon located in the lower Mojave River drainage of the western Mojave Desert, California [119]. Historically, the fire interval in this mesquite-saltbush (_Prosopis_ spp.-_Atriplex confertifolia_) dominated environment was <35 to <100 years [127,145]. However, the infestation of the drought-deciduous saltcedar (_Tamarix ramosissima_) has altered the historic fire regime, promoting a fire interval of about 10 to 20 years [119]. Mountain rush grows on rough fescue-Parry's oatgrass dominated grasslands in Banff and Jasper national parks, Alberta. Prior to national park designation, fires were "frequent" and "extensive" especially in the main valleys. For the last 80 years, however, a fire control policy has been put in place, and while fires are still frequent, they are small and mostly in grassland, scrub, or forest litter areas close to human activity [185].

## Fire Management Considerations

Since mountain rush re-establishes quickly after fire [8,82,150,206,214,215,220,220], prescribed fire can be used as a management tool to promote its growth. Some research does suggest, however, that while fire does not completely destroy mountain rush seed banks, it does have a detrimental effect on them [34,207]. If fire is chosen as a management tool for mountain rush, managers should be cognizant of potential negative effects on associated or surrounding vegetation. For instance, mountain rush is a dominant species in Afton Canyon located in the lower Mojave River drainage of the western Mojave Desert, California. In the past several decades the area has been infested by the drought-deciduous saltcedar which is highly fire tolerant and may expand after disturbances such as fire and severely reduce native plant coverage [119].

## Importance to Wildlife and Livestock

**Livestock:** Mountain rush is described as a fair to good forage species for cattle [21]. While mountain rush is primarily used as a source of food, one study in southeastern Alberta found that mountain rush stands averaging 225 13-inch (33-cm) stalks per foot² was a favorite bedding material of cattle [104].

A study located on upper Big Grizzly Creek in Plumas County, California, found that mountain rush was an important forage species for steers. The 10-acre (4-ha) grazing meadow was fenced into ten 1-acre (0.4-ha) plots and allotted as no-, low-, or moderate-grazing. The designation low- (1,500 kg/ha) and moderately- (1,000 kg/ha) grazed were characterized by the amount of standing biomass left on the plots at the end of the grazing experiment. On low-grazed plots, mountain rush composed 15.8% and 13.8% of steers' diet in 1992 and 1993 respectively, while on moderately grazed plots it made up 14.6% and 15.2% of their diet, respectively [94].

A study of cattle grazing preferences in a montane riparian community found that while mountain rush made up 2.2% to 5.5% of available biomass it constituted 2.0% to 14.0% of cattle's diet. ### Cover Value

Mountain rush is an important cover species for a variety of small birds, upland game birds, birds of prey, and waterfowl [20,31,36,37,38,42,56,69,104,122,128,129,130,131,137,160,169,190,191]. The yellow rail, a small marsh bird found along the St. Lawrence River, Quebec, uses mountain rush stems as a primary nest material. A study conducted in 1993 found that mountain rush was present in 67% of their nests [163]. An extensive study of small impoundments in southeastern Alberta, an area dubbed the "duck factory" of North America, found that 62% of all waterfowl species located their nests in mountain rush communities. A total of 78% of mallards use mountain rush in the impoundment areas as their primary nesting cover. ## Value for Rehabilitation of Disturbed Sites

Mountain rush's production of deep and fibrous roots emanating from a mass of coarse and creeping rhizomes makes it a valuable species for stabilizing streambanks and protecting against soil erosion [21,77,78,153]. Based on samples taken at 3 sites in northwestern Nevada and 2 sites in northeastern California, streambank particle erosion rate was moderate in mountain rush streambank communities [58].

Mountain rush is being used by the Utah Division of State Lands and Forestry for restoration projects involving the construction of wetland ponds. Optimal growth of mountain rush plugs occurred when water levels were 1 to 2 inches (3-5 cm) below the soil surface [13].

In 1978 a dike enclosing a 52-acre (21-ha) pasture along the north shore of the Salmon River estuary in northwestern Oregon was removed by the United States Forest Service, allowing the land to return to a salt marsh. In surrounding salt marsh communities mountain rush is a dominant species. Data were collected for 10 years in the restored and surrounding (control) salt marshes to assess mountain rush's ability to return to natural numbers without reintroduction by humans. While mountain rush did not reach mean percent cover and mean percent frequency values found in the control plots in 10 years, it did increase from 1978 levels [67]:

Saltcedar and small-flowered tamarisk (_Tamarix parviflora_) have extensively invaded Red Rock Canyon State Park, California. By late summer of 1987, less than 6 months after the initial treatment, mountain rush began to germinate in the newly open spaces. The populations of mountain rush resulted from native seed without intervention by humans [64].

## Other Uses

The stems of mountain rush have been used by the Chumash Indians of southwest California as a foundation for coiled basketry [193]. Mountain rush can also be used to make baskets and containers by the Kawaisu Indians of California and the Northern Cheyenne Indians of Montana [85,222]. The Northern Cheyenne also use the fine rootlets of mountain rush to sew patterns on robes or other leather items [85].

### Grazing

Mountain rush is generally described as an increaser under grazing pressure [40,77,78,94,113,201,202,203]. At the Malheur National Wildlife Refuge, Oregon, a 1.2-acre (0.5 ha) plot with a history of annual haying was placed in nonuse for 3 growing seasons while the surrounding area continued to be hayed. In November 1978, a third of the nonuse area was subjected to grazing for 1 growing season, another third was left unused, and the final third was burned (See Fire Effects). At the end of 1 growing season a vegetative analysis was taken and found the rush species (mountain rush and Sierra rush) composition was 12.0% in the nonuse plot, 20.0% in the annually hayed plot, and 22.6% in the grazed plot [42]. At Wimena National Forest in central Oregon, mountain rush increased under grazing. A Kentucky bluegrass dominated meadow that had been grazed by cattle from April through October for the past 50 years was partially fenced off from grazing in April 1967. In July 1977, average canopy cover of mountain rush on sites continually grazed since 1967 was 5.6% while on protected sites it was 4.7% [201].

Data from a study site located on upper Big Grizzly Creek in Plumas County, California indicated that mountain rush was both an increaser and a decreaser under various grazing pressures. The 10-acre (4 ha) grazing meadow was fenced into ten 1-acre (0.4 ha) plots and allotted as no, low, or moderate grazing. The designation low (1,500 kg/ha) and moderately (1,000 kg/ha) grazed were characterized by the amount of standing biomass left on the plots at the end of the grazing experiment. Experimental plots were established in 2 locations, mid-meadow and streamside. In 1992 at the mid-meadow site, mountain rush's growth rate was significantly (p<.05) lower in the low and moderately grazed treatments compared to the ungrazed site. At the streamside location, growth rate of mountain rush was similar in the moderately- and ungrazed plots which were both significantly greater than the low-grazed plot. In 1993 there was no significant difference between grazing and nongrazing treatments at the mid-meadow site, but at the streamside location mountain rush growth was significantly greater on the moderately-grazed plot than on the low- and no-grazed plots which were not significantly different. Differences across treatments and years is likely attributable to changes in soil moisture retention [94].
