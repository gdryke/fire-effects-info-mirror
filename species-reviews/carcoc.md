# Carex concinna — low northern sedge

**Written:** April, 1994
  
**Contributors:** Roberta A. Walsh - 1st Author

## Introduction

## Taxonomy

The currently accepted scientific name of low northern sedge is _Carex concinna_ R. Br. [5,8,9]. It is in the family Cyperaceae. There are no accepted infrataxa.

### Synonyms

 * No entry

### Other Common Names

beautiful sedge

## Distribution

## General Distribution

Low northern sedge occurs from Newfoundland and Quebec west to Alaska [5,9]. From Quebec it extends south to northern Michigan and northeastern Wisconsin [5,7]. From Alaska it extends south to Oregon and then east to South Dakota and Colorado [1,7,8,9].

### States and Provinces

 * United States: AK CO ID MI MT ND OR SD WA WI WY
 * Canada: AB BC MB NB NF NT ON PQ SK YT

## Site Characteristics

Low northern sedge is found in forests [10], open woods [3], and clearings [9]. It occurs on stony, dry [8,10], often calcareous soils [5,9,10,15].

[![A sedge plant growing on rocky soils.]({{ site.baseurl }}/assets/images/species-reviews/feis-carcoc_plant.jpg)Photo Credit Photo by nambec, iNaturalist (CC BY-NC). Low northern sedge growing in Quebec, Canada.](<https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nc/4.0/>)

In the Rocky Mountains and Colorado Basin, low northern sedge is found in rich, peaty soils chiefly in calcareous areas at elevations of 5,000 to 11,000 feet (1,524-3,353 m) [9].

## Plant Communities

Low northern sedge was not listed as an indicator or dominant in available literature.

Low northern sedge in Michigan is found at the edges of northern white-cedar (_Thuja occidentalis_) and balsam fir (_Abies balsamea_) thickets at the northern ends of Michigan and Huron lakes. Associated species include bristle-leaved sedge (_Carex eburnea_), hairlike sedge (_C. capillaris_), twinflower (_Linnaea borealis_), and bearberry (_Arctostaphylos uva-ursi_) [15].

## Botanical and Ecological Characteristics

## Botanical Description

Low northern sedge is a native, perennial, monoecious graminoid [8]. It is loosely caespitose [5]. Culms are 2 to 8 inches (5 to 20 cm) tall [1] and triangular [8]. Leaves are mostly basal, five to nine per culm [9], 2 to 4 inches (5 to 10 cm) long, and 0.04 to 0.12 inches (1-3 mm) wide [7]. Inflorescences are both terminal and lateral and 0.12 to 0.28 inches (3 to 7 mm) long [5]. The achene is 0.06 inches (1.5 mm) long [8]. The perigynia surrounding the achene is obtusely triangular and 0.12 inches (3 mm) long [1]. Low northern sedge has slender, scaly, often long rhizomes or stolons [1,9,10].

## Seasonal Development

Low northern sedge blooms in June and July in the north-central and northeastern United States and adjacent Canada [5].

## Regeneration Processes

Low northern sedge sprouts from perennating buds at the base of the culms [8] and from rhizomes [10]. It also reproduces by seed [9].

## Fire Ecology and Management

## Immediate Fire Effects

Low northern sedge culms are probably killed by fire during the growing season.

## Postfire Regeneration Strategy (Stickney 1989)

 * Rhizomatous herb, rhizome in soil
 * Tussock graminoid

## Fire Adaptations

Since low northern sedge can reproduce vegetatively [8], it probably sprouts from rhizomes after aerial portions are burned. Where thick tufts form, they may protect basal buds from fire-caused damage.
