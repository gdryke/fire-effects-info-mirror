## Taxonomy

The currently accepted scientific name of little larkspur is _Delphinium bicolor_ Nutt. It is in the family Ranunculaceae [5,8,9,10,12]. There are no recognized subspecies, varieties, or forms.

### Synonyms

 * No entry

### Other Common Names

low larkspur, montane larkspur

## Distribution

## General Distribution

Little larkspur is distributed from Alberta and Saskatchewan south through northeastern Washington, Idaho, Montana, western North Dakota, western South Dakota, western Nebraska, and Wyoming [8,9,10,12].

### States and Provinces

 * United States: ID, MT, NE, ND, SD, WA, WY
 * Canada: AB, SK

## Site Characteristics

Little larkspur is found on sites ranging from open woods and grasslands to subalpine scree [5,9,10,12]. It appears early in the spring, often at the edges of receding snowbanks [3,18]. Little larkspur will grow in fairly dry to moist conditions [3,4,18]. It grows best in rich, black, sandy loams or clay loams and in soils of limestone or granitic origin [3,4]. It is found on gentle to steep slopes. Little larkspur is found at elevations of 4,600 to 11,600 feet (1,400-3,500 m) in Wyoming and 3,000 to 10,500 feet (900-3,200 m) in Montana [4].

## Botanical and Ecological Characteristics

## Botanical Description

Little larkspur is a native, perennial forb with an extensively branched fibrous to slightly fleshy root system. Stems are usually solitary and are 4 to 16 inches (10-40 cm) tall. Fruits are many-seeded follicles. Seeds are irregularly winged and about 0.08 inches (0.2 cm) long [8,9,10,12].

### Raunkiaer Life Form (Raunkiaer 1934)

 * Hemicryptophyte

## Seasonal Development

Little larkspur begins growth as soon as snow melts in the spring. It flowers from June to July [17].

## Regeneration Processes

Little larkspur mainly reproduces by seed [17]. It is pollinated by bees, and probably self-pollinates as well [1]. Little larkspur may reproduce vegetatively [4].

## Successional Status

Little larkspur prefers sites with full sun exposure [18]. It colonizes recently disturbed sites [4] and is often found on gravel banks and along roadcuts [8]. On alpine tundra sites of the Beartooth Plateau, Montana, little larkspur is associated with natural ground disturbance, such as areas of rodent activity [1].

## Fire Ecology and Management

## Immediate Fire Effects

Little larkspur is probably killed by most fires.

## Postfire Regeneration Strategy (Stickney 1989)

 * Secondary colonizer - off-site seed
 * Ground residual colonizer (on-site, initial community)

## Fire Adaptations

Information on adaptations of little larkspur for survival following fire is not available in the literature. Larkspur species seeds may survive in the soil seedbank [18]. If little larkspur reproduces vegetatively [4], it may sprout after fire.

## Plant Response to Fire

Information regarding the response of little larkspur following fire is lacking in available literature. Little larkspur was present in postfire years 1 and 3 following an August wildfire in ponderosa pine (Pinus ponderosa) stands in Idaho [13].

## Importance to Wildlife and Livestock

Little larkspur is utilized by elk, mule deer, white-tailed deer, pronghorn, upland game birds, small nongame birds, and small mammals [4].

Little larkspur is very poisonous to cattle [12].

### Palatability and Nutritional Value

The palatability of little larkspur is rated fair for sheep and poor to fair for cattle and horses [4].

Little larkspur is poor in protein and energy value [4].

### Cover Value

The palatability of little larkspur is rated fair for sheep and poor to fair for cattle and horses [4].

## Value for Rehabilitation or Restoration of Disturbed Sites

No entry.

## Other Uses

Larkspur species are cultivated as ornamentals [3]. Crushed larkspurs were used by Native Americans for controlling lice and other insects [17].

## Other Management Considerations

Little larkspur is toxic to cattle and causes some losses in the spring and early summer. However, it dries rapidly and senesces after seed set. It is, therefore, probably safe to graze after seed set [3,14,17,18].

Little larkspur is less toxic and more palatable to sheep than cattle. It is considered fairly good forage for sheep [3,18].

Little larkspur increases in response to grazing [20].
