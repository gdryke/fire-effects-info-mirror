## Introduction

## Taxonomy

The currently accepted scientific name for mapleleaf viburnum is _Viburnum acerifolium_ L. [11]. Two intergrading varieties are recognized: V. a. var. _acerifolium_ and V. a. var. _glabrescens_ Rehd. [2].

### Synonyms

No entry.

### Other Common Names

dockmackie, mapleleaved arrow-wood, possum-haw, squash-berry, guelder-rose

## Distribution

## General Distribution

Mapleleaf viburnum occurs from southern Ontario to Quebec, south to eastern Texas, and east to the northern panhandle of Florida [5,9,24].

### States and Provinces

 * United States: AL AR CT DE FL GA IL IN IA KS LA ME MD MI MN MS MO NE NJ NY NC OH OK PA RI SC TN TX VA WV WI
 * Canada: ON PQ

## Site Characteristics

Mapleleaf viburnum occurs in upland forests, woodlands, ravine slopes, and hillsides [12,15,21]. It occurs in well-drained, moist soils and is particularly tolerant of acid soils [4,9].

Common understory associates of mapleleaf viburnum include witchhazel (Hamamelis virginiana), mountain laurel (Kalmia latifolia), eastern hophornbeam (Ostrya virginiana), low sweet blueberry (Vaccinium angustifolium), Virginia creeper (Parthenocissus quinquefolia), sassafras (Sassafras albidum), and striped maple (Acer pensylvanicum) [15,17,25].

## Plant Communities

Mapleleaf viburnum is a dominant or codominant understory species in many beech-maple (Fagus-Acer) forests in the northeastern and midwestern United States [6,17,25].

## Botanical and Ecological Characteristics

## Botanical Description

![Photo of mapleleaf viburnum.](https://research.fs.usda.gov/sites/default/files/styles/large/public/2025-06/feis-vibace_plant.jpg?itok=RLHAx08y)

Photo Credit

Mapleleaf viburnum.

Mapleleaf viburnum is a large, deciduous, rhizomatous shrub from 3 to 6 feet (1-2 m) tall [11,24]. It has a straight trunk with spreading, ascending branches, and forms dense thickets. The maple-like leaves are 3 to 5 inches (7.5-12.5 cm) long. The flowers are arranged in flat upright clusters. The fruit is a one-seeded drupe [4,10].

### Raunkiaer Life Form (Raunkiaer 1934)

 * Phanerophyte

## Seasonal Development

Mapleleaf viburnum flowers from May to August, depending on location. Fruits ripen from July to October [9,11].

## Regeneration Processes

Mapleleaf viburnum begins to produce seed at approximately 2 years of age, and produces large amounts of seed every year. The seed is dispersed by animals and by gravity [9].

Most mapleleaf viburnum seeds have an impermeable seedcoat and exhibit embryo dormancy that requires a warm-cold stratification sequence to be broken [9].

Mapleleaf viburnum probably reproduces vegetatively by rhizomes [19].

## Successional Status

Mapleleaf viburnum is a mid- to late-seral species [16,19]. It is shade tolerant and requires partial shading for optimum growth and development [9,14].

## Fire Ecology and Management

## Immediate Fire Effects

Presumably, fire top-kills the aerial portions of mapleleaf viburnum.

## Postfire Regeneration Strategy (Stickney 1989)

 * Rhizomatous shrub, rhizome in soil
 * Initial-offsite colonizer (off-site, initial community)

## Fire Adaptations

Mapleleaf viburnum is not well adapted to fire. Fire is harmful to mapleleaf viburnum at both short and long return intervals [3].

Presumably, low- to moderate-severity fires top-kill mapleleaf viburnum. It probably survives fire by sprouting from underground rhizomes.

## Plant Response to Fire

Following a prescribed fire in a pine-mixed woodwood forest in Ontario, mapleleaf viburnum decreased in both frequency and biomass [20].

## Importance to Wildlife and Livestock

The fruits of mapleleaf viburnum are eaten by white-tailed deer, rabbits, mice, skunks, ruffed grouse, ring-necked pheasants, wild turkeys, and many species of songbirds [1,9]. The twigs, bark, and leaves are eaten by white-tailed deer, moose, rabbits, and beavers [9].

### Palatability and Nutritional Value

No entry.

### Cover Value

The dense undergrowth of mapleleaf viburnum provides good nesting and escape cover for numerous species of birds and small mammals [9,24].

## Value for Rehabilitation or Restoration of Disturbed Sites

No entry.

## Other Uses

Mapleleaf viburnum has been cultivated since 1736 for its attractive flowers and foliage [24].
