## Summary

This review synthesizes the information that was available in the scientific literature as of 2024 on the biology, ecology, and effects of fire on masked bobwhites, an endangered subspecies of the northern bobwhite with a disjunct distribution in the Southwest. Masked bobwhites are nonmigratory, year-round resident birds with historical distributions in south-central Arizona and northern Sonora, Mexico. As of the 21st century, their distribution is limited to a few disjunct sites. One site is the Buenos Aires National Wildlife Refuge in southeastern Arizona, which was established to facilitate recovery of masked bobwhites. The other two sites occur in north-central Sonora on privately-owned ranches.

Masked bobwhites require relatively open-canopied habitats with dense, tall grasses and forbs interspersed with woody cover, particularly mesquite. Semidesert grasslands are critical habitats for masked bobwhites. Habitat is also provided by desert scrub, thorn scrub, and “brushy areas” within desert riparian areas, bottomlands, drainages, and agricultural areas when they are adjacent and intermixed with semidesert grasslands. All the plant communities used by masked bobwhites share a common habitat structure of abundant herbaceous plants and sparse to moderate woody plants in the understory that provide food and protective cover for refuge and retreat.

Masked bobwhites typically breed in summer, coinciding with the onset of monsoons and warm temperatures. Breeding may also occur in late winter and early spring if precipitation and temperature are high enough. The breeding season typically lasts only about 90 days, which is thought to be too short for multiple clutches or renesting to occur. Masked bobwhites nest on the ground in dense clumps of vegetation that conceal the nest overhead. Warm-season perennial bunchgrasses are particularly important nesting substrates. Young are precocial and capable of leaving the nest shortly after hatching. Broods average 11 chicks. Masked bobwhites eat primarily seeds, fruits, and insects and require access to diverse foods. They forage on the ground, scratching lightly through scattered litter and vegetation for food or gleaning it from substrates that can be reached below about 0.2 m from the ground.

Habitat management for masked bobwhites focuses on creating and/or maintaining abundant and diverse herbaceous cover and sparse to moderate woody cover, which are lacking in most masked bobwhite habitats. Prescribed fire can be a tool for creating and maintaining high-quality masked bobwhite habitats, but prescribed fire alone is typically insufficient to meet habitat management objectives, and fires that are too frequent reduce the abundance of critical native sprouting woody plants. In addition, nonnative invasive plants such as Lehmann lovegrass and buffelgrass are likely to increase after fire. Instead, protecting critical habitat areas from fire, or using infrequent prescribed fire (≥15- to 20-year intervals) combined with practices that reduce nonnative invasive plants, minimize soil disturbance, and enhance the reestablishment of native shrubs and grasses (e.g., by seeding or planting) may improve masked bobwhite habitat.

The masked bobwhite is a subspecies of the northern bobwhite (_Colinus virginianus_). It is genetically, phenotypically, morphologically, and geographically distinct from other northern bobwhite subspecies [36,111,113]. Masked bobwhites are more closely related to Texas bobwhites (_Colinus virginianus texanus_) from southern and southwestern Texas and Mexican subspecies than they are to any of the other subspecies in the eastern United States [111,112]. Masked bobwhites exhibit the lowest genetic diversity among seven northern bobwhite subspecies in the United States [36]. Information on the genetic variability of captive and wild masked bobwhites and comparison with other northern bobwhite subspecies is reviewed in the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service’s 5-year review of the species’ endangered status [99].

For information on other subspecies of northern bobwhite, see the FEIS Species Review about the northern bobwhite.

## General Distribution

Historical accounts and scientific collections indicate that masked bobwhites were restricted to the Plains of Sonora subdivision of the Sonoran Desert [21,67], from south-central Arizona south to the Rio Yaqui in northern Sonora. In Arizona, it occurred in an area bounded to the east by the Baboquivari Mountains, to the west by the Santa Cruz Valley, and to the north by the Altar Valley [15,56,67,102] (fig. 2). Masked bobwhite populations are geographically disjunct from other northern bobwhite subspecies, being effectively isolated by the Chihuahuan Desert [15,113].

Masked bobwhites were extirpated from Arizona in the early 1900s and subsequently reintroduced multiple times [15] (see Population Trends). As of the 21st century, the distribution of masked bobwhites is limited to three disjunct sites. One site is the 48,000-ha Buenos Aires National Wildlife Refuge (BANWR) located in the Altar Valley in southeastern Arizona, which was established in 1985 to facilitate recovery of masked bobwhites [67,90]. Reintroduced masked bobwhites currently occur in and around the refuge [6]. The other two sites occur in north-central Sonora on privately-owned ranches: the Rancho El Carrizo near Benjamin Hill, Sonora, approximately 120 km south of the BANWR, and the Rancho Grande 20 km south of Rancho El Carrizo [67]. A 2013 to 2015 survey found little evidence of masked bobwhites at El Carrizo and other locations near Benjamin Hill [46], although they were heard in the area as recently as 2011 [99]. More recent surveys were not available at the time of writing this review. All three sites are considered marginal habitat for masked bobwhites [20] (see Site Characteristics).

## Plant Communities

Photo by George Gentry, U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, and courtesy of the National Digital Library. 

Figure 3—Semidesert grasslands at the Buenos Aires National Wildlife Refuge, Arizona. 

Masked bobwhites require relatively open-canopied habitats with dense, tall (50-100 cm) annual and perennial grasses and forbs interspersed with woody cover, particularly mesquite. Semidesert grasslands (also called Sonoran savanna grasslands) are critical habitats for masked bobwhites [5,17,20,21,67,90,96,102]. They are dominated by perennial grasses or a combination of annual and perennial grasses [69]. Semidesert grasslands used by masked bobwhites are described as “productive” [90], “lush” [67], and “rank” with grasses and forbs [20]. Common grasses and forbs include various species of grama (e.g., Rothrock’s grama, needle grama, slender grama, and Parry’s grama), threeawns, sand dropseed, big sacaton, bush muhly, Arizona cottontop, false grama, tanglehead, vine-mesquite, ragweed, purslane, spurges, pigweed, and spiderling [18,20,21,92]. Trees, shrubs, and cacti are sparse and include mesquite, acacia, creosotebush, burroweed, and pricklypear, among others [69]. In reintroduced masked bobwhite home ranges at the BANWR, the most frequently occurring grass species were threeawns, dropseeds, and gramas, including Rothrock’s grama, as well as Arizona cottontop. The most frequently occurring forb species were weakleaf bur ragweed, Russian-thistle, and Thurber's pepperweed. Mesquite was also common [92].

Desert scrub, thorn scrub, and “brushy areas” associated with desert riparian areas, bottomlands, and drainages as well as pastures and agricultural areas provide additional habitats for masked bobwhites when adjacent to or intermixed with semidesert grasslands [5,17,20,21,67,90,96,102]. Desert scrub communities have clumped and patchy canopies of small trees, tall shrubs, and large cacti. Dominant overstory plants of desert scrub habitats used by masked bobwhites include mesquite, acacia, desert ironwood, paloverde, and succulents such as organpipe cactus, barrel cactus, cactus apple, cholla, and ocotillo. Perennial grasses or forbs may dominant the understory [69,81]. Common trees and shrubs in masked bobwhite habitats near Benjamin Hill include mesquite, desert ironwood, paloverdes, Jerusalem thorn, guayacan, acacias, Mexican groundcherry, copper caesalpinia, Sonoran croton, spiny hackberry, kidneywood, babybonnets, Mexican tree ocotillo, nettlespurge, fairyduster, and sensitive peas [18].

Sonoran desert riparian communities are flood-dependent ecosystems that occur as a mix of riparian woodlands, shrublands, and grasslands. Dominant species are Fremont cottonwood, willows, mesquite, arrowweed, saltgrass, alkali sacaton, sedges, and cattails. Acacia may be prevalent in the midstory and tree canopy [69].

Historically, the eastern and southern boundaries of the masked bobwhite’s distribution were limited by the transition of Sonoran semidesert grasslands to denser Sinaloan thorn scrub, and the western and northern boundaries of its distribution were limited by the transition to drier desert scrub. At higher elevations, its distribution was limited by warm temperate desert grasslands dominated by sod-forming perennial grasses [56,67,102].

## Site Characteristics

Historical accounts and scientific collections indicate that masked bobwhites occurred on level plains and river valleys as well as foothills at low to moderate elevations [20,21,56,67]. They did not occur in canyons or arroyos [18]. Scientific collections collected between 1884 and 1992 from 251 locations suggest that masked bobwhites occurred from 240 to 1,060 m elevation [20], while a 2012 review concluded that they had a slightly broader elevational range occurring from 150 to 1,200 m [67].

Masked bobwhites occur in tropical and subtropical climates [20,21], on sites that experience regular drought [25]. Mean annual temperatures are greater than 18 °C [20]. Freezing temperatures are infrequent, and most do not exceed 24 continuous hours [21]. Annual precipitation within the historical masked bobwhite range is bimodal [25,90,96] and ranges from 317 to 542 mm with mean summer precipitation (June–September) usually >200 mm [20]. On the BANWR, precipitation averages 420 mm annually with approximately 40% occurring during July and August and the remaining during winter. Precipitation varies along a north to south gradient, with relatively cooler and wetter conditions in the southern portion of the BANWR. May to September temperatures average 33 °C and December to February temperatures average 18 °C [90]. At Rancho El Carrizo, annual precipitation averaged 371 mm from 1956 to 1994 and showed considerable variability within and among years (min–max: 171–687 mm). Mean monthly rainfall peaked from July to August, when 53.5% of the annual precipitation occurred. A second peak occurred from December to January, when 16.8% of average annual precipitation occurred [25]. From 1932 to 1971 near Benjamin Hill, average annual rainfall was 343 mm, with 75% falling from July to September. Another precipitation peak occurred in December and January with 11% of the annual rainfall [95].

An analysis of masked bobwhite habitat characteristics suggests that the last remaining sites with masked bobwhites are marginal habitat because they occur at the northern (BANWR) and southern (Rancho El Carrizo) extremes of the species’ historical range. In addition, these sites are relatively high in elevation (BANWR) or relatively low in average annual precipitation (Rancho El Carrizo; 344 mm) compared with sites in other parts of its historical range [20]. For example, at the BANWR, elevations range between 925 m to 1,400 m, which is at the upper elevational limit for masked bobwhites [47,90].

Most of the semidesert grasslands at the BANWR are now dominated by nonnative invasive grasses. Lovegrasses, particularly Lehmann lovegrass but also weeping lovegrass and Wilman lovegrass, currently dominate most upland sites at the BANWR [90]. In Sonora, habitats have been extensively modified by mechanical removal of native vegetation and planting of nonnative invasive buffelgrass [99] (see Threats).

## Biological Characteristics

This review includes information covering many aspects of the masked bobwhite’s life history and focuses on those most relevant to fire. Reviews important to this effort include that of Hernandez et al. (2006) [56] and Brown and Clark (2017) [19] as well as reviews of masked bobwhite ecology and conservation by Tomlinson (1972) [96] and Kuvlesky et al. (2000) [67].

Because much of the biology and ecology of the masked bobwhite is poorly understood, information about other northern bobwhite subspecies is used to supplement information about masked bobwhites. Much of this information comes from a literature review about northern bobwhites in Birds of the World by Brennan et al. (2020) [15].

### Species Description

The masked bobwhite is a small- to medium-sized quail about 15 cm tall with a short neck and tail [15,53]. The male’s plumage is considered the most physically distinct of the northern bobwhite subspecies. It is distinguished by a black head and throat and largely solid cinnamon-rufous breast and belly. Its back is mottled brown. Most males have some white on the head, particularly through the eye and occasionally on the throat. The female’s plumage is drabber than the male’s and resembles that of other female northern bobwhite subspecies, especially the Texas bobwhite, although the upper back and flanks of female masked bobwhites tend to be more rufous [15,67,96,102,113].

Little information is available in published literature on masked bobwhite body size. Early studies found wings of seven adult males from northern Sonora and Arizona (location not specified) averaged 108 mm long (min–max: 103–111 mm) and tails averaged 63 mm long (min–max: 61–67 mm) [1], while near Benjamin Hill, 31 males had an average wing length of 113.5 mm and 21 females averaged 112.9 mm. Weight averaged 168.6 g for 26 adult males and 162.8 g for 19 adult females [97].

#### Social Structure

Masked bobwhites are highly social and live in groups called coveys during fall, winter, and spring. Covey size typically ranges from 10 to 16 individuals [29], averaging about 12 birds, and rarely exceeding 20 birds [21,95], although Tomlinson (1972) reported one covey observed in 1942 that contained more than 30 birds [96]. Coveys are comprised of individuals from both sexes and various ages. Breeding pairs form between individuals within a covey. The timing of covey formation and dissolution varies by year and with precipitation (see Breeding and Nesting). When breeding pairs form, pairs gradually separate from coveys. Loose coveys containing several pairs and single males are common at this time. Once breeding begins, coveys dissolve and individuals occur in family groups that consist of a breeding pair and their offspring (i.e., a brood) [18,21,79,102]. Parents care for the young for about 5 weeks after hatching [15,53] (see Incubation, Hatching, and Brooding), then individuals assemble once again into coveys. Nonreproductive adults and young separated from their broods may join a covey [21].

#### Life Span and Mortality

##### Life Span and Mortality Rates

Life span of wild masked bobwhites is not known. Some released masked bobwhites have survived 2.5 years [63].

Annual adult and juvenile mortality rates for wild masked bobwhites are not reported but are thought to be high [29], and perhaps similar to that of other northern bobwhite subspecies [67]. In population dynamic models, juvenile (from fledging to 1 year old) mortality was set at 70% based on observations of Texas bobwhites and other northern bobwhite subspecies. Annual adult (>1 year old) mortality was set at 65% for both sexes. Juvenile mortality was the most influential variable in masked bobwhite population dynamics in a model that included average age of first reproduction, age of senescence, offspring production, sex ratio, adult mortality, juvenile mortality, initial population size, carrying capacity, individual supplementation, individual reduction for supplementation, and drought and other occasional catastrophic events [29].

##### Sources of Mortality

###### Predators

Predation may have a substantial impact on masked bobwhite populations [67]. Raptors—particularly red-tailed hawks and northern harriers, but also Cooper's hawks and Swainson's hawks—are considered the main masked bobwhite predators [14,67]. Mammals are also important predators, with coyotes and bobcats likely killing adults, and northern raccoons likely killing eggs [67]. Other mammalian predators include common gray foxes, ringtails, white-nosed coatis, Virginia opossums, and skunks [14]. As with other northern bobwhite subspecies, it is likely that nearly every opportunistic terrestrial predator within the range of the masked bobwhite will prey on eggs, young, and adults [15].

###### Diseases and Parasites

A 1996 review of threats to masked bobwhites summarizes major diseases and parasites that have been documented in wild and captive masked bobwhites or are of specific concern because of their potential to affect the populations if introduced through captive propagation and release, translocation, or transmission from other host species. These include avian adenoviruses, avian poxvirus, and coccidiosis [14]. Captive flocks at the BANWR have been prone to disease outbreaks that have caused large-scale mortality [99].

###### Weather/Exposure

In Sonora, anecdotal information gathered from ranchers in the Benjamin Hill area, indicated that Hurricane Lester in 1992 may have killed masked bobwhites that had congregated at relatively higher locations in the valley to avoid high water and were subsequently preyed upon by raptors. Hurricanes and intense monsoonal storms impact the area regularly and can produce standing water during the masked bobwhite’s nesting season that could inundate and kill eggs. Observations by BANWR staff of captive masked bobwhites held in outdoor pens indicated that birds die when temperatures drop below about -7 °C or when cold and rainy conditions occur together [99].

### Development and Reproduction

Little quantitative data are available regarding masked bobwhite life history. Masked bobwhite breeding activity, nesting, and brood-rearing behavior, as well as covey formation, are thought to follow patterns like those reported for other northern bobwhite subspecies in the United States [56,67,96], especially those of the Texas bobwhites [67]. However, the timing of breeding activity for masked bobwhites differs from that of other subspecies, with masked bobwhites exhibiting a bimodal breeding season initiated and maintained largely by precipitation [67].

#### Breeding and Nesting

##### Mating System

The mating system of the masked bobwhite is like that of other northern bobwhite subspecies. Both males and females incubate and raise young with more than one mate during the breeding season [15].

##### Breeding and Nesting Season

Masked bobwhites exhibit a bimodal breeding season initiated and maintained largely by precipitation [67], which occurs mostly in summer but also winter [90] (see Site Characteristics). Most breeding activity coincides with the onset of summer monsoons [67] and warm temperatures [95]. This is a much shorter breeding season than that of other northern bobwhite subspecies in the United States [56] (see below). Breeding activity may also occur in late winter and early spring if precipitation and temperature are high enough [67,95]. Humidity levels must exceed 90% for masked bobwhites to initiate breeding activity [67]; thus, masked bobwhites may not breed if precipitation is too low [79]. In Sonora, masked bobwhite males began calling (indicating the onset of breeding activity) after minimum daily temperatures reached 13 °C and relative humidity reached 25% [95].

According to reviews based largely on information from Sonoran populations (e.g., [25,96]), males typically begin calling and pairs begin forming in late June, with pair bonds forming while still within coveys as early as March. Pairs do not display breeding behavior until rainfall begins in mid-to-late July [18,21,56,67,102]. Peak breeding activity generally occurs in August and ends when humidity decreases in September [25,67]. Initial nest incubation begins in late July and peak hatching occurs in early to mid-September. Hatching ends during late October or early November [96].

Masked bobwhites may also breed in late winter and early spring during years of above-average winter precipitation. Juvenile masked bobwhites have been observed in some locations during late March and early April following wet winters, indicating that breeding likely occurred during late February or early March [67]. At the BANWR, the “pair formation/breeding season”, (i.e., early spring and summer breeding seasons combined), is variously described as occurring from April or May to September or October [45,59,92].

The summer breeding season is shorter than other northern bobwhite subspecies in the United States [56]. The masked bobwhite breeding season typically lasts only about 90 days, assuming that breeding only occurs after the onset of summer monsoons. In eastern United States subspecies, the breeding season typically lasts about 150 days [56] and birds can produce two or, rarely, three clutches per year [15,53]. Northern bobwhites, in general, require 47 to 55 days from nest construction to hatching their first clutch, and require 20 to 34 days between clutches [53]. If timing is similar for masked bobwhites, the short duration of the summer breeding season (90 days) may not be enough for masked bobwhites to produce more than one clutch and reduces the likelihood that they will have enough time to nest after an initial nest failure. This substantially reduces the reproductive potential and associated productivity of masked bobwhites as compared to other northern bobwhite subspecies [99].

##### Clutch and Brood Size

Masked bobwhite broods average 11 chicks (min–max: 5–15 chicks/brood) [21,95]. Information about the number of eggs per nest and sex ratios for masked bobwhites is lacking; however, northern bobwhites lay 12 to 15 eggs, have high hatch rates, and a 50:50 sex ratio at hatching [29].

##### Incubation, Hatching, and Brooding

Masked bobwhite incubation and hatching behavior is similar to that of other northern bobwhite subspecies. Males and females incubate and care for their young [15]. Hatching within nests is synchronous, usually occurring within 1 day. Young are precocial and capable of leaving the nest shortly after hatching [15,53]. Young can procure food and grit on their first day of life [15].

Chicks are hatched with natal down that does not provide efficient protection from the elements. They are also poor thermoregulators. Young must be brooded (i.e., kept warm by a parent) intensively until they are capable of thermoregulation. Both sexes brood young. Frequency of brooding behavior in parents gradually decreases over time. Young less than 1 week old are brooded for most of the day, with parents leaving only for short periods to forage. At 2 weeks old, young are capable of partial thermoregulation. By about 5 weeks old, they can thermoregulate and no longer require intensive brooding by adults [15,53].

##### Fledging and Growth

Growth and development of young is rapid. Immature masked bobwhites gain an average of approximately 85 to 95 g in about 3 months [97]. By 2 weeks old, they are capable of short flights of 12 to 15 m. Juvenile plumage is complete at 8 weeks old, with sexual dimorphism apparent by 8 to 10 weeks. Adult plumage predominates by 12 to 13 weeks. Individuals are usually considered physically mature based on plumage and size at 15 weeks old [53].

#### Movements

Studies of radiotagged, reintroduced masked bobwhites, and studies of other northern bobwhite subspecies, indicate that northern bobwhites are generally sedentary but can travel long distances (>2 km) [92]. Individuals typically remain near their birthplace [15,92]. Movement appears to increase in fall when coveys form and again in spring when they dissolve [92]. In winter, masked bobwhites may move extensively in search of food [18]. When protective cover is insufficient, birds may disperse. For example, at the BANWR, several coveys dispersed from a pasture when grass-forb cover was reduced by grazing to 400 kg/ha and 12 to 15 cm tall; the move made birds more vulnerable to raptor predation (Goodwin 1982, cited in [65]).

#### Reproductive Success

##### Age at First Breeding

Males and females may attempt to breed in their first year after hatching [29,53].

##### Reproductive Success

Masked bobwhite reproductive success and population size appears to be reduced during dry years [21,95] and during years with very high and very low temperatures [56,99], although actual reproductive rates are not reported in published literature. Extremely hot temperatures can limit northern bobwhite reproduction by reducing egg fertility or production, shortening the length of the nesting season, and reducing the amount of thermally suitable habitat, and may have similar impacts on masked bobwhite populations [56]. Masked bobwhite population declines were associated with dry weather during a 28-year call-count survey in Sonora. Population estimates declined in 13 of 14 years when the preceding 3-year average of June to August rainfall was <200 mm and increased in 11 of 13 years when the preceding 3-year average was >200 mm, suggesting a threshold of ≈200 mm of average precipitation from June to August over 3 years for population growth [25]. At Rancho El Carrizo, masked bobwhite populations reached a 30-year high during a year of record high rainfall (1998), but populations were near zero during one of the driest years on record (2002) [19]. Texas bobwhites in mixed deciduous thorn scrub communities had lower survival and reproductive effort during a drought year (510 mm precipitation), with a lower proportion of females nesting (53%), lower nesting rates (1.2 nests/female), a shorter nesting season (69 days), and lower productivity (69% juvenile in fall harvest) compared with a wet year (930 mm) (100%, 2.3 nests/female, 159 days, and 78%, respectively) [55]. If masked bobwhites respond to drought in a similar manner, then drought could be a limiting factor in masked bobwhite reproduction [56].

Early spring breeding activity likely does not occur every year due to insufficient winter precipitation most years. Thus, subsequent recruitment rates are lower during early spring than during summer. However, periodic early spring breeding activity is likely important to population maintenance and growth. Models indicate that the summer masked bobwhite breeding season is too short to sustain a masked bobwhite population without additional—even if only occasional—recruitment during spring [67].

## Activity and Habitat

Masked bobwhites spend much of their time on the ground, typically flying only short distances to avoid disturbance and predators [102]. Like other northern bobwhite subspecies, they forage primarily during the day but are most active in the early mornings and late afternoons until dark to avoid high temperatures during midday. Because masked bobwhites are relatively sedentary [92], they require food and protective cover in relatively close proximity [32]. Dense herbaceous cover intermixed with woody cover are critical habitat components for masked bobwhites because they provide food, a suitable microclimate, and protection from predators [15,80,102].

### Diet and Foraging

#### Diet

Although few studies report on the diet of masked bobwhites, their diet appears to consist mostly of seeds (particularly hard-coated seeds from perennial grasses, forbs, and leguminous trees and shrubs) and arthropods (primarily insects) [56,67,102]. They also eat green plant material, but that may be less important [102]. Similarly, annual grasses do not appear important ([90], LaRoche and Conway 2013, cited in [115]). An October study of the contents of 10 masked bobwhite stomachs from Sonora reported a diet consisting of 79.1% plant material and 20.1% animal material. Seeds were from prairie acacia (18.8%), groundcherry (16.3%), panicgrasses (14.6%) and other grasses (3.4%), whitemouth dayflower (10.8%), Santa Rita Mountain bean (3.8%), Indian mallow (2.9%), sensitive partridge pea (2.5%), and morning-glory (2.3%). Animal material consisted primarily of grasshoppers (18.4%) but also other Orthoptera, stinkbugs, cutworms, beetles, ants, and flies (2.5%). In Arizona, stomach contents of masked bobwhites contained parts of prairie acacia, whitethorn acacia, fairyduster, snakewood, and screwbean mesquite [105] although information on the types of plant parts or on the timing of collection was not provided. On the BANWR, the contents of approximately 20 crops of young masked bobwhites consisted mostly of insects and forbs [67] but no details were provided on species taken or time of collection. Young consume “smaller sized insects” than adults, the size of which appears to increase as they age [99], although the actual size of insects consumed is not reported. Foye et al. (2015) reported that arthropods eaten by adult northern bobwhites are from about 5 to 10 mm long, with those between about 2 and 5 mm long considered most suitable for chicks [42].

While evidence is limited, masked bobwhite’s diet likely changes seasonally and with sex and age [102]. Unpublished data from an unspecified location suggest that seeds make up 60% of their diet in spring and up to 80% in fall. During spring and summer, adults increase their consumption of arthropods, up to about 25%. While young consume arthropods exclusively for their first few weeks. Green plant material, such as that of legumes, makes up the smallest part of the masked bobwhite’s diet (James et al. 2015, cited in [102]) and is likely most important in summer and early fall [21]. Leguminous shrubs and subshrubs such as prairie acacia and sensitive partridge pea appear to be particularly important in the diet in winter [90].

Nonnative invasive grasses may reduce food available to masked bobwhites. Lehmann lovegrass and buffelgrass are nonnative species that have substantial negative impacts on masked bobwhite habitats [56] (see Contemporary Fire Regimes). These species do not provide food for masked bobwhites [66]. For example, Lehmann lovegrass seeds are too small for masked bobwhites to eat [99]. Furthermore, grasshoppers, a primary food source for masked bobwhites, were 44% less abundant on plots dominated by Lehmann lovegrass and/or weeping lovegrass than native grassland plots [11]. In southeastern Arizona, species richness of arthropods decreased nearly 2% and total abundance decreased nearly 7% for every 10% increase in Lehmann lovegrass cover. Orthoptera richness and abundance decreased by 1% and 2%, respectively, for every 10% increase in Lehmann lovegrass cover [4].

#### Water and Grit Requirements

Masked bobwhites must drink water daily or obtain it from their food [102]. In general, surface water is not essential for northern bobwhites, and water needs are usually met by water found in food, although surface water may be used if it is available, and its availability may influence the species’ distribution [15,35].

While grit is considered not essential for northern bobwhites to grind hard seed food [15], Cottam and Knappen 1939, cited in [96]) reported that gravel formed 11.4% of the stomach contents of 10 masked bobwhites collected in Sonora in October, indicating that grit may be readily consumed by masked bobwhites.

See Brennan et al. (2020) [15] for information on nutrition and energetics of northern bobwhites that may be relevant to masked bobwhites.

#### Foraging Activity

Masked bobwhites forage on the ground, scratching lightly through scattered litter and vegetation for food, or gleaning food from substrates that can be reached to about 0.2 m from the ground (such as insects on plant stems and leaves) [15,71,104]. Seeds that fall in a dense mat of litter or vegetation are inaccessible to foraging northern bobwhites [35], and arthropods—even if abundant—may be inaccessible if vegetation is too dense for the birds to travel through [7]. Masked bobwhites forage primarily during the day. They are often more active in the early mornings and late afternoons to avoid high temperatures [15,80,102].

#### Foraging Habitats

Northern bobwhites are relatively “weak scratchers” and cannot forage in dense (>5 cm deep) litter or vegetation, so bare ground and sparse litter that can be moved easily are important in foraging habitats (e.g., [61,93,104]). Northern bobwhites also require sparse vegetation cover below about 0.2 m (the approximate height of a masked bobwhite) that is sufficiently easy for the birds to walk through and some overstory protection from predators above that height [35,71,93].

Little information is available specifically about masked bobwhite foraging habitats. Reviews noted that plant communities with mixed gramas and threeawns with adjoining brushy areas are preferred for feeding and loafing, while plant communities with more densely tufted perennial grasses like sacaton are used for escape and/or protective cover. Gramas dominate plains, while sacaton is generally found in sandy bottomlands [21,56,96]. One study found that water content of vegetation was higher at foraging sites (≈140 g/m2) used by broods than at loafing (≈40 g/m2) and roosting (≈60 g/m2) sites. The authors speculated that succulent vegetation provided a higher relative abundance of insects (Taylor and Guthery 1994, cited in [53]).

### Nest Sites

Masked bobwhites nest on the ground [79,102]. Like other northern bobwhite subspecies, both sexes select the nest site, and they build the nest together. Senesced herbaceous growth from the previous growing season and current year’s growth are both important at nests sites. Senesced herbaceous growth is used to construct the nest itself and is often woven into an arch above the nest to conceal it [15,60]. In southwestern Texas, Texas bobwhites "preferred to locate nests in clumps of grass containing considerable green material." Nest linings, however, were constructed almost exclusively of dead or dry grass (Lehmann 1946, cited in [31]). Nest-building takes approximately 5 days. Nests are about 15 cm wide and about 5 cm deep [15].

Little information was available regarding nest sites specifically of masked bobwhites. In general, masked bobwhites require dense herbaceous cover to conceal the nest, and nesting may not occur until sufficient cover is available [18,21]. A review noted that northern bobwhites select nest sites with dense vegetation cover shorter than 45 cm tall within 15 to 20 m of an opening [15]. Another review about northern bobwhites concluded that warm-season, perennial bunchgrasses provide excellent nesting cover; forbs, shrubs, and cacti, such as pricklypear, also provide important nesting cover [53]. Masked bobwhites may nest in buffelgrass clumps (W. Kuvlesky 2005, personal communication cited in [99]), but Lehmann lovegrass creates a dense thatch that is unsuitable for nesting [99]. Anecdotal information suggests that when nonnative grasses are used as nest sites, they have poor hatching success (L. Johnson, personal communication cited in [91]).

### Brood-rearing Habitats

Little information was available regarding brood-rearing habitats of masked bobwhites. In Arizona, masked bobwhites shift from relatively open grasslands in late summer and early fall during nesting to savanna margins with taller growth during brood rearing. The tall growth provides protective cover as well as forage such as insects, green shoots, leaves, and flowers of legumes and other forbs. In late fall, broods move into shrubbier habitats [18]. A review noted that northern bobwhites select brood-rearing habitats with shade provided by shrubs and tall herbaceous plants (≈40 cm), open herbaceous cover providing good visibility and movement, and green, growing vegetation that provides abundant food items [53]. Broods in southern Texas used microsites with higher woody cover during midday for loafing sites (≈30%) compared to feeding (≈5%) or roosting (≈15%) sites (Taylor and Guthery 1994, cited in [53]). Northern bobwhite broods in Kansas selected roost sites with taller vegetation (44.8 cm) and visual obstruction to a greater height (51 cm) compared to nearby habitat patches (36.3 cm and 38 cm, respectively). The researchers hypothesized that these characteristics might have provided favorable nighttime microclimates for young birds that had not yet reached thermal independence (Taylor et al. 1999, cited in [53]). In southern Texas, vegetation height and visual obstruction at roost sites decreased with increasing age of broods, with broods initially roosting in tall, dense vegetation such as shrubs, but then progressively roosting in more open habitats such as grasslands as they matured (F. Hernandez, unpublished data cited in [53]). See the FEIS Species Review about northern bobwhites for more information.

### Roosting Sites

During the night, masked bobwhites usually roost on the ground [21], but like other northern bobwhite subspecies they may occasionally roost in shrubs or other vegetation above ground [15]. Northern bobwhites, in general, roost in a circle or "rosette" in a group, with tails pointing inward and beaks outward—often in direct contact—presumably to conserve energy and/or detect predators [15]. Little information was available regarding specific roosting sites of masked bobwhites. Like other northern bobwhite subspecies [73], masked bobwhites appear to select areas with dense vegetation for roosting [21]. The type of roosting cover used at different locations is probably dependent on such factors as an individual’s age (see above), severity of winter, habitat composition, and the intensity and kind of predation [68,73]. Northern bobwhites generally leave the roost shortly after sunrise to begin feeding [32].

### Protective Cover for Refuge and Retreat

Masked bobwhites, like northern bobwhites, require protective cover that provides refuge from predators and weather (e.g., cold or hot temperatures), and retreats for loafing (resting) during mid-day and roosting at night. Preferred patches of protective cover provide a visual screen from aerial and ground predators between about 0.2 and 1.0 m above the ground while having a relatively open vegetation cover below 0.2 m that permits ease of movement [15,35]. Protective cover is typically provided by woody plants or a combination of woody plants, tall grasses and forbs, and/or cacti [35,72,94]. Woody cover is typically sparse to moderate in masked bobwhite habitats [49,59,102]. See Habitat Composition and Structure for more information on type and arrangement of protective cover used by masked bobwhites. Masked bobwhites may also take short flights to nearby trees for protective cover. Goodwin and Hungerford (1977) observed flushed masked bobwhites perching 5 to 10 m above the ground in nearby trees. They may also fly longer distances, for example from 100 to 500 m, into grasslands to find protective cover [49].

### Area Requirements

Only one study reported information on area requirements of masked bobwhites. In this study, home ranges for reintroduced masked bobwhites at the BANWR averaged 10.9 ha (min–max: 5.2–14.6 ha), and home range sizes were not different between breeding and covey seasons. Home ranges had an average maximum width of 586 m, except for one pair that travelled 2.4 km from their home range and returned the following day. Core areas (i.e., areas where 50% of locations were aggregated) averaged 1.1 ha (min–max: 0.2–2.7 ha). Masked bobwhite mean home range size was consistent with home range sizes reported for other subspecies of northern bobwhites [92] (see the FEIS Species Review about northern bobwhites). The only nesting female masked bobwhite in the study restricted her activity to about 2% of the total home range while nesting. Home ranges of nesting pairs overlapped [92].

Habitat Composition and Structure

Masked bobwhites prefer open habitats with relatively tall, dense herbaceous vegetation [18,49,65], especially of perennial bunchgrasses ([59], Mills and Reichenbacher 1982, cited in [92]), and low to moderate amounts of woody cover [49,51]. Their habitats have relatively high vegetative structural complexity [59] and relatively high plant species diversity [49,65] compared to other available habitat. Patches of dense herbaceous vegetation and woody plants are important for providing not only abundant and diverse food, but also cover and visual obstruction. Cover above a height of about 0.2 m (the height of a masked bobwhite), but is open underneath, is ideal for protecting masked bobwhites from predation while allowing for ease of travel [35,50,51,59,67,71,93,102]. Due to the importance of both herbaceous and woody vegetation, masked bobwhites require landscapes with an interspersion of grass-forb and shrub-tree habitat [49,92,95] and often use edge habitats [20,49,56,95,115]. Masked bobwhites have been described as an “edge species” [26].

Most masked bobwhite habitats in the Altar Valley have been degraded by historically heavy livestock grazing, establishment and spread of invasive plants, increased frequency of fire, and past management practices that removed too much herbaceous and woody cover [51,67,90,99,115,115]. Bottomlands, drainages, washes, and associated ecotones provide some of the best remaining habitats, providing both abundant and diverse herbaceous vegetation and woody cover [49,65,84]. On BANWR from 1979 to 1981, masked bobwhites almost exclusively used the bottomlands of main and side drainages. They occurred most often in habitats with 22% to 30% grass-forb cover, 18 to 20 species of grasses and forbs, and shrub cover of 15% to 30%. They avoided habitat with <10% grass-forb cover and <8% shrub cover, and, with few exceptions, masked bobwhites did not occur in areas with less than 10 to 12 species of grasses and forbs. Monocultures—even those comprised of important food species such as vine-mesquite—were avoided. Mesquite with a basal diameter <10 cm and with low, pendulous branches close to the ground appeared ideal masked bobwhite habitat; larger mesquite provided little protective cover. Masked bobwhites seemed to prefer areas with 500 to 1,000 kg/ha of grasses and 300 to 800 kg/ha of forbs; however, the number of plant species present and amount of vegetation cover provided were more important than plant biomass in masked bobwhite habitat selection. Several coveys emigrated from home ranges when the grass-forb understory was reduced by grazing to 400 kg/ha and 12 to 15 cm height (Goodwin 1982, cited in [65]). A later (1986–1987) study on the BANWR found masked bobwhite habitat was comprised of 38% grass cover, 11% forb cover, and 8% tree and shrub cover, and at least 10 species of grasses and forbs. The following habitat requirements for masked bobwhites at the BANWR are based on habitat use of radiomarked birds in predominately bottomland sites [92]:

 1. Approximately 11 ha per covey or pair.
 2. An interspersion of grass, grass-forb, and shrub habitat.
 3. Diversity of grasses and forbs (10 species of each).
 4. 150 trees or shrubs/ha in the 0–5 m height class.
 5. 90% visual obstruction by vegetation at the 0–1 dm height interval.
 6. 50% grass cover.
 7. 30% basal grass cover.
 8. 15% forb cover.
 9. 10% tree and/or shrub cover.
 10. Absence of half-shrubs.
 11. Absence of Lehmann lovegrass.

Limited data on masked bobwhites reintroduced into the Altar Valley of Arizona from 1975 to 1980 indicated that masked bobwhites used areas with relatively high canopy cover of woody and herbaceous vegetation (75%–100%). These included bottomlands; edge habitats where mesquite-lined washes are next to open grass-forb sites; areas with dense grass-shrub "pockets”; and lovegrass-grama flats [49].

Masked bobwhites use habitats with low to moderate amounts of woody cover. Woody cover provides protection from predators and forms microsites that moderate temperature extremes and produce more humid conditions [49,59,102]. According to a review, masked bobwhite habitat should consist of at least 20% to 25% woody cover, and ideal shrub height is about 1-m tall [67]. At the BANWR and Rancho El Carrizo, masked bobwhites selected habitats with an average of 17.5% and 17.0% woody cover, respectively. They used patches with higher woody cover during midday (when they would be loafing) than during morning or evening (when they would be feeding). There was a tendency for masked bobwhites to use patches with relatively lower woody cover during fall and winter than during spring and summer, possibly due to heat avoidance during spring and summer. Operative temperatures (at 15 cm above ground; a measure of temperature experienced by the bird) and exposure to aerial predators were lower at sites used by masked bobwhites than random available sites [51]. Habitat models of woody cover, bare ground cover, exposure to ground predators, exposure to aerial predators, and operative temperature indicated that masked bobwhites at Rancho El Carrizo used sites with a broader range of conditions than masked bobwhites at the BANWR and that the acceptable range of any specific variable was dependent on values for other variables. For example, at Rancho El Carrizo, the acceptable range for woody cover was broad (0%–60%) when all other variables were at acceptable values. However, as other variables trended away from optima, the acceptable range for woody cover constricted (15%–40%) [52].

Low to moderate cover of the nonnative invasive perennial grasses buffelgrass and Lehmann lovegrass may provide suitable masked bobwhite habitat, but high nonnative grass cover lacks important food for masked bobwhites [64,66]. At the BANWR during covey season, the frequency of occurrence of Lehmann lovegrass was higher at masked bobwhite sites (59.4% of total grasses) than at available sites (36.4%), but during the breeding season, frequency of occurrence of Lehmann lovegrass was similar between masked bobwhite sites (31.9%–36.2%) and available sites (33.7%–40.0%), suggesting that remaining areas of native grass at the BANWR may not provide sufficiently dense structure for masked bobwhites during winter [59]. In Rancho Grande, masked bobwhites were more common in pastures with buffelgrass than pastures with native annual grasses. The buffelgrass pastures averaged 63% grass cover (primarily buffelgrass), 11% forb cover, and 11% woody cover, while the native grasslands were 1% perennial grass cover, 1% perennial forb cover, and 33% woody cover. They did not use extensive monocultures of buffelgrass but used areas where buffelgrass was mixed with other grasses and forbs (Mills and Reichenbacher 1982, cited in [92]). In addition, northern bobwhites have been observed in high abundance on Texas rangelands characterized by moderately dense stands of buffelgrass interspersed with native grasses, forbs, and open ground [56]. In a review of the impact of nonnative invasive grasses on quail in the Southwest, Kuvlesky et al. (2002) noted that while masked bobwhites use plant communities with Lehmann lovegrass and buffelgrass, particularly during drought and when native plant communities are lacking, these plant communities are likely useful only as protective cover, and thus, food must be located nearby [66].

## Fire Ecology and Management

## Fire-caused Mortality

No information was available in the published literature on fire-caused mortality of masked bobwhites. Once masked bobwhites can fly, direct fire-caused mortality is unlikely, although fires could kill some masked bobwhites unable to escape fast-moving fires [99]. Because masked bobwhites nest directly on the ground in dense vegetation, eggs and nests are vulnerable to even low-intensity fire. Fire is reported to have killed northern bobwhite eggs and adults (e.g., [8,37,48,62,75,75,89,106]). For more information, see the FEIS Species Review about northern bobwhites.

## Fire Effects on Habitats

The effect of fire on masked bobwhite habitat changes with time since fire. Fires reduce important food and protective cover for masked bobwhites in the short-term [10,95,110]. At the BANWR, masked bobwhites were absent during the breeding season following a “hot”, 200-ha April or May prescribed fire intended to reduce woody cover. Nine to 12 birds were observed in the area prior to the fire [95]. However, grass and forb abundance in semidesert grasslands can recover quickly after fire (e.g., [10,12]) suggesting that fire may benefit masked bobwhites in 2 or 3 postfire years [115]. However, recovery time depends on plant and fire characteristics, land use, topography, soils, and precipitation, among other factors [47,90,115]. Plant communities used by masked bobwhites primarily include semidesert grasslands but also adjacent and intermixed desert scrub, thorn scrub, and desert riparian areas (see Plant Communities). No information was available regarding fire effects on masked bobwhites in these other plant communities.

Much of the potential masked bobwhite habitat in the BANWR has been burned at least once since the refuge’s establishment in 1985, and several areas have been burned from three to five times between 1985 and 2015 (either under prescription or by wildfire) [90]. In some areas, fire frequency (3- to 5-year intervals) is higher than estimates of historical fire frequency [115] (see Historical Fire Regimes). Such frequent burning appears to reduce masked bobwhite habitat suitability. Models suggest that habitat suitability increases as fire becomes less frequent and time-since-fire lengthens. The most suitable habitats occur where fires were relatively infrequent or absent (i.e., have not burned in ≥30 years). These areas include narrow portions of drainages that had low plant productivity and fine fuels and/or were intentionally protected from fires. The least suitable habitats were often dominated by Lehmann lovegrass, had high fine-fuel loads, and burned frequently (as often as 3 to 8 years). Except in the drainages, trees and leguminous shrubs important to masked bobwhites were not widely distributed and had very low cover due to frequent fires and past management practices [91,115]. While woody cover was higher in unburned areas than frequently burned areas, forb cover was greatest on plots burned once and declined with increasing fire frequency [90]. Researchers concluded that frequent fire on the BANWR degraded habitat suitability because woody plants require relatively long fire intervals for recruitment and they became too sparse. Fire intervals between 15 to 20 years may be needed to develop the matrix of grass and shrub habitat required by masked bobwhites [90,91].

Some woody plants important to masked bobwhites sprout after fire (e.g. mesquite and acacia) [90,115]. For example, mesquites are typically top-killed and sprout after fire, resulting in a multi-stemmed “shrub-like form” that is thought to provide better protective cover for masked bobwhites than tall, single-stemmed mesquite trees [99]; however, low-growing, multiple-stemmed mesquite resprouts were rarely used as protective cover by northern bobwhites in the Texas Rolling Plains when larger trees were available [86]. In addition, mesquite populations gradually decline with very frequent fire (<10-year intervals) [78,90,91].

Nonnative perennial grasses, such as Lehmann lovegrass and buffelgrass, may recover quickly and increase in abundance after fire, often dominating postfire communities (e.g., [3,13,58,88,90,100]). Dominance of semidesert grasslands by these invasive grasses increases fire risk (see Contemporary Fire Regimes and Fuels).

## Fire Regimes

### Historical Fire Regimes

Masked bobwhites occur primarily in semidesert grasslands that are adjacent to or intermixed with desert scrub, thorn scrub, and “brushy areas” associated with desert riparian areas, bottomlands, and drainages (see Plant Communities). Historical mean fire frequency estimates based on LANDFIRE succession modeling range from 8 to 82 years in semidesert grasslands and steppes [69] (table 1), and likely varied depending on factors that influence soil moisture availability, including precipitation, topography, and soils [91]. Frequent fire likely maintained sparse woody cover in semidesert grasslands, and longer fire intervals likely promoted later stages of vegetation development dominated by shrubs with little to no perennial grasses or forbs. When fires occurred, they were predominantly replacement or mixed severity [69] (table 1). Fires were ignited by dry lightning during summer monsoonal storms (mid-May to early September), when lightning is common [16,38,69,83]. Mean historical fire intervals in adjacent communities based on LANDFIRE succession modeling ranged widely, from <10 to >1,000 years (see LANDFIRE Biophysical Settings section). ### Contemporary Fire Regimes and Fuels

Today, wildfires in masked bobwhite habitats are ignited by both lighting—often associated with the North American monsoon—and anthropogenic sources [109]. Fire regimes in plant communities in the southwestern United States and in Sonora changed with European-American settlement and the introduction of livestock grazing and nonnative invasive plants (e.g., [14,28,65]). In the late 1800s, heavy livestock grazing greatly reduced dense grassland fuels that historically carried fire [95]. This reduction of grasses and forbs, coupled with major droughts, eliminated the masked bobwhite's protective cover, food, and nesting habitat and contributed to masked bobwhite extirpation in Arizona by 1900 (see Population Trends). The lack of fine fuels prevented fires from igniting and spreading, and in combination with fire exclusion, allowed woody plants such as mesquite to spread from drainages into adjacent semidesert grasslands to a level considered unsuitable for masked bobwhites [14,65].

In the 1970s, prior to the establishment of BANWR, trees were removed from nearly all land with moderate topography in the Altar Valley, and large areas were seeded with Lehmann lovegrass to improve livestock forage [115]. After the BANWR was established in 1985, livestock grazing ceased and fire—both prescribed fire and wildfire—occurred at higher frequencies than historically occurred in many areas (as frequent as 3- to 5-year intervals during 30 years) [90,115]. Prescribed fires were mostly conducted in late spring [90] but also early summer [99], and the wildfire season is typically from April to June during the hot, dry period prior to monsoonal moisture [107]. However, masked bobwhite habitats did not return to historical conditions after these changes [47]. Rather, many areas are dominated by nonnative invasive grasses and lack sufficient woody cover [47,91,115]. In Sonora, heavy livestock grazing resulted in similar ecological effects, with reduced herbaceous cover and fine fuel loads and increased abundance of thorn scrub-associated woody plants [65,99].

Lehmann lovegrass and buffelgrass are abundant and widespread nonnative species that have substantial negative impacts on native plant communities within the range of masked bobwhites [56] (see Nonnative Invasive Plants), and other nonnative plants, such as other lovegrasses [5], Johnsongrass [92] (fig. 8), and yellow bluestem [5], may be locally abundant. Lehmann lovegrass is rhizomatous and forms a denser, more continuous fuel bed than the native perennial bunchgrasses that dominated semidesert grasslands historically [27,70,76]. It can dominate semidesert grasslands, altering species composition, reducing species richness and diversity, and reducing structural complexity [3,14,90]. Fuel models developed for BANWR predict higher fire line intensity and larger fires in areas dominated by Lehmann lovegrass than in areas dominated by native fuels [33]. At the BANWR, frequent fires led to higher fuel loads comprised of Lehmann lovegrass [90] and consequent high fire risk in masked bobwhite habitats [110]. Fire behavior simulations indicate that Lehmann lovegrass establishment and spread could create high fine-fuel biomass that can result in large, intense fires [33], which would further reduce suitable masked bobwhite habitat [90]. The BANWR continues to maintain an active prescribed fire program to ameliorate the risk of wildfire due to abundant fine fuels resulting from Lehmann lovegrass invasion [109]. See the FEIS Species Review about Lehmann lovegrass.

Buffelgrass is a nonnative perennial grass that was intentionally introduced on cattle ranches in Sonora in the 1970s [74]. Between 1990 and 1998, the Mexican government subsidized cattle ranchers to convert native desert and thorn scrub to buffelgrass pastures [23], and as of 2006, as many as 1.6 million ha had been seeded to buffelgrass in Sonora [43]. The vast conversion of native communities to buffelgrass pasture facilitated the spread of buffelgrass into native communities of the Sonoran Desert. Buffelgrass invasion can increase the abundance and continuity of persistent fine fuels that can burn at greater fire intensity relative to native plant communities, as has been observed in the Sonoran Desert [39,77]. It is commonly suggested that buffelgrass increases fire hazard and fire risk in the Sonoran Desert (e.g., [2,30,34,39,44]). See the FEIS Species Review about buffelgrass.

## Fire Management Considerations

Habitat management for masked bobwhites focuses on creating and/or maintaining abundant and diverse herbaceous cover and low to moderate woody cover [56,79]. However, suitable herbaceous and/or woody cover are lacking in most masked bobwhite habitats ([51,59,67], LaRoche and Conway 2012, cited in [90]). For example, in Sonora and the BANWR, herbaceous cover for foraging and nesting and shrub cover necessary for thermoregulation, predator avoidance, and winter food are deficient [51]. Nine experts on masked bobwhite ecology indicated that a lack of leguminous shrubs that are important for winter food was likely limiting masked bobwhite abundance at the BANWR. Masked bobwhites prefer habitats with tall, dense herbaceous understory and low to moderate woody cover with high structural complexity and high plant species diversity relative to nearby habitats (see Habitat Composition and Structure).

Prescribed fire can be a tool for achieving many of these recommended habitat conditions. For example, fire can increase abundance of grasses and forbs important to masked bobwhites in the short term and stimulate sprouting of important woody plants (see Fire Effects on Habitats). Prescribed fire has historically been the primary management tool at the BANWR to improve habitat for masked bobwhites [26]. However, prescribed fire alone is typically insufficient to meet habitat management objectives for masked bobwhites [115] and fires that are too frequent are detrimental [90,91] because abundance of important native sprouting woody plants is likely to be reduced by very frequent fire (see Fire Effects on Habitats). In addition, nonnative invasive plants such as Lehmann lovegrass and buffelgrass are likely to increase abundance after fire. For this reason, prescribed fire alone is not recommended on sites where Lehmann lovegrass [100] or buffelgrass [40] occurs. Instead, protecting critical habitat areas from fire, or using infrequent prescribed fire (≥15- to 20-year intervals), combined with practices that reduce Lehmann lovegrass and buffelgrass, minimize soil disturbance, and enhance the reestablishment of native shrubs and grasses (e.g., by seeding or planting) may improve management outcomes over prescribed fire alone [90,115,115]. The Masked Bobwhite Recovery Plan (1984, revised 1995) recommends prescribed fires, if used, be conducted on a rotation with various times-since-fire to produce a mosaic pattern of habitat types within a masked bobwhite home range [65].

### Fire Frequency

In semidesert grasslands, long fire intervals promote dense cover of woody shrubs and trees at the expense of perennial grasses and forbs. On the other hand, very short fire intervals prevent woody species from recovering. Over the past century, mesquite has increased abundance in these grasslands due to livestock grazing and fire exclusion [9,14,65]. Since the establishment of BANWR, management and frequent fire have reversed this trend to the degree that woody plants used by masked bobwhites for food and protective cover are too sparse. In areas of the refuge where ≥2 fires burned over the last 30 years, fire intervals between 15 and 20 years may promote the matrix of grass and shrub habitat required by masked bobwhites [90,91] (see Fire Effects on Habitats).

### Fire Season

No information is available about how fire timing affects masked bobwhites. Because masked bobwhites nest directly on the ground, eggs and nests are likely vulnerable to even low-intensity surface fire, which suggests that prescribed fires that occur during the breeding season (mostly summer) may harm birds. Masked bobwhites are unlikely to renest after disturbances because the breeding season is short [99] (see Breeding and Nesting Season). Prescribed fires in masked bobwhite habitats have typically occurred in late spring [90] but also early summer [99].

### Fire Size

A study of prescribed fire effects on masked bobwhite habitats hypothesized that relatively large fires may increase distances to unburned, dense vegetation and make large areas uninhabitable for masked bobwhites [115].

When prescribed fire was first introduced at the BANWR, 6,000 to 8,000 ha were burned annually and burn blocks were typically 400 to 2,500 ha. With the completion and signing of the Habitat Management Plan in 2010, total burn area became limited to 3,600 ha/year, with burns within the masked bobwhite zone limited in size to 20 to 40 ha. As of 2014, large burns were still planned for other refuge program goals such as restoration of pronghorn habitat [99]. More recent published information on prescribed fire size was not available.

### Integrated Management with Fire

In western rangelands where invasive grasses are of concern, herbicide and reseeding may be used in conjunction with prescribed fire to reduce the dominance of invasive plants [108]. See the USDA’s Field Guide for Managing Lehmann and Weeping Lovegrasses in the Southwest (2014), which details strategies for combining treatments to contain and reduce lovegrass dominance, including a prescribed fire-herbicide strategy. This strategy is primarily recommended in areas with a near monoculture of lovegrasses, and fire is used to remove litter and growth prior to herbicide application. Reseeding these areas after treatments is also recommended to restore them to native plant species. Because lovegrasses recover quickly after fire, prescribed fire alone is not recommended to control these species [100]. See also the FEIS Species Review about Lehmann lovegrass.

Control of buffelgrass requires removing established plants and reducing propagule pressure [24,41] by preventing seed production, depleting the buffelgrass soil seed bank, and establishing and maintaining desired vegetation [24,85]. The Field Guide for Managing Buffelgrass in the Southwest (2017) recommends repeating treatments (e.g., manual removal and chemical control) annually, and possibly within a single growing season, for several consecutive years to deplete the buffelgrass seed bank [103]. Because buffelgrass recovers quickly after fire, prescribed fire alone is not recommended to control it [40]. See also the FEIS Species Review about buffelgrass.

## Nonfire Management Considerations

## US Federal Legal Status

In the United States, masked bobwhites have been federally listed as endangered since 1968 [56,101].

## Mexican Federal Legal Status

In Mexico, masked bobwhites have been listed as endangered since 1994 [90].

## Other Status

According to NatureServe, masked bobwhites are critically imperiled in Arizona [79].

## Population Trends

Masked bobwhites were extirpated from the United States by about 1900 probably because of habitat degradation caused by overgrazing by cattle and drought [18]. Tens of thousands of masked bobwhite individuals have since been released from captive-rearing programs [26,52], and a small number of reintroduced masked bobwhites currently occur in the wild in and around the BANWR [6]. Population estimates of masked bobwhites in 2023 suggest approximately 200 individuals occur on the refuge [102], but an active captive-rearing program in the United States and Mexico is critical to maintaining wild populations [98]. The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service’s 5-year review of the species’ endangered status (2014) [99] concluded that masked bobwhite populations at the BANWR are not self-sustaining. See Hernandez et al. (2006) for information on distribution, population size, and recovery efforts for masked bobwhites, including history of translocations and releases of captive-reared birds [56]. Only a few populations may exist in Sonora, but their status is poorly known [79]. A survey for masked bobwhites in Sonora from 2013 to 2015 failed to observe any individuals [46]. The status of masked bobwhite populations in the United States and Mexico is considered “precarious at best” [56] and captive populations remain critical to their conservation and recovery [98].

## Threats

Habitat loss and alteration resulting in insufficient native herbaceous and woody cover appears primarily responsible for masked bobwhite population declines [20,99,102]. In Arizona, about 90% of low-elevation semidesert grasslands has been lost or degraded due to various landscape-level impacts such as overgrazing by livestock, decreased fire frequency from fire exclusion, prolonged drought, and residential and commercial development. Among these, nonnative invasive grasses, particularly Lehmann lovegrass and buffelgrass, pose the greatest threat [5] (table 2). Both species were intentionally seeded as livestock forage after mechanical shrub removal in masked bobwhite habitats [74,99,115]. The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service’s 5-year recovery assessment stated that habitat conditions are not and have never been optimal for masked bobwhites at the BANWR because leguminous shrubs that characterize high-quality masked bobwhite habitat are nearly absent in the valley bottom portion of BANWR, where masked bobwhites reside. The historical extent of habitats suitable for masked bobwhites at the refuge is unknown, but the prescribed fire program established in 1985, as well as mechanical treatments to remove shrubs, has likely contributed to reducing the presence and/or size of shrubs in the valley bottom to the masked bobwhite’s detriment (S. Dobrott, J. Levy, R. Tomlinson 2004, personal communication cited in [99]). In addition, masked bobwhites have low reproductive potential (see Breeding and Nesting), which likely has contributed substantially to declines [56].

Masked bobwhites may use habitats with low to moderate nonnative invasive grass cover, but they avoid highly invaded areas. Masked bobwhites are likely to avoid highly invaded areas because they lack abundant forbs and arthropods and have low plant species diversity and structural complexity (e.g., [32,54,56,59,65,66,99]) (see Habitat Composition and Structure). In addition, masked bobwhites movements are restricted in dense vegetation, such as in monocultures of buffelgrass [56]. For example, masked bobwhite numbers declined [67,99] as Rancho El Grande became a near monoculture of buffelgrass. Furthermore, masked bobwhites probably use plant communities dominated by buffelgrass and Lehmann lovegrass only as protective cover and not as food. Therefore, their use depends on the proximity of food [66].

Additional threats to masked bobwhites include climate change (see Management Under a Changing Climate), diseases in the captive population, lack of genetic variation, predation, and habitat fragmentation [98].

## Nonfire Habitat Management

Management to improve habitat for masked bobwhites includes any practice that increases cover of woody vegetation and height and cover of native herbaceous vegetation, including preventing the establishment of and/or controlling nonnative invasive plant species. See Brown and Clark (2017) for a synthesis of masked bobwhite habitat and population restoration efforts [19]. See also Kuvlesky et al. (2000) [67] and Cohan et al. (2012) [26] for earlier reviews of masked bobwhite recovery efforts. See Sesnie and Dickson (2018) [90] for a summary of masked bobwhite recovery efforts and BANWR management history.

### Nonnative Invasive Plants

Lehmann lovegrass (in BANWR) and buffelgrass (in Sonora) are abundant and widespread nonnative invasive species that have substantial negative impacts on native plant communities within the range of masked bobwhites [56], although other nonnative plants, such as other lovegrasses [5], Johnsongrass [92] (fig. 8), and yellow bluestem [5], may be locally abundant. Controlling nonnative invasive species may increase the quality and quantity of plant communities for masked bobwhites.

Recommended actions for managing nonnative invasive plants in masked bobwhite habitats include:

 * Implementing livestock management practices that reduce grazing pressure on native grasses.
 * Applying herbicide and mechanically removing new nonnative invasive plants.
 * Establishing native grass stands.
 * Preventing introduction of nonnative invasive plants [5].

### Modifying Shrub and Herbaceous Cover

In most masked bobwhite habitats, shrub cover is limiting ([51,59], LaRoche and Conway 2012, cited in [90]), but in localized areas, shrub cover may be too tall or too dense [26]. In areas where shrub cover is limiting, reestablishing native shrubs is important to masked bobwhites [65]. In areas where woody canopy height is too tall, “half cutting” (bending partially cut branches to the ground) is recommended for masked bobwhites [65]. No additional information was provided regarding its efficacy. Increasing habitat heterogeneity and promoting herbaceous plants in areas where shrubs are too dense has been accomplished by mechanically removing shrubs and constructing brush piles [26].

On degraded upland sites, planting native plant species and aerating the soil have been used to improve foraging and nesting habitats for masked bobwhites [26]. On overgrazed sites, mechanical treatments that fracture the soil surface and permit water infiltration may promote herbaceous cover for masked bobwhites. Anecdotal observations in Sonora suggest that discing and aerating the soil improved the quantity and diversity of herbaceous cover but researchers noted that treatments must be applied such that ample amounts of mature woody cover remain after treatment [51]. Such treatments have been used and studied on the BANWR since 2005, but results have not been published. Preliminary monitoring suggests increased forb abundances, “more robust” native perennial grass clumps, and longer periods of green vegetation in aerated areas compared with adjacent untreated sites (BANWR, unpublished data cited in [99]). In Sonora, contour berm construction has been used since 2005 to improve herbaceous growth for masked bobwhites by increasing water penetration and retention [99].

### Livestock Grazing

Overgrazing by livestock was a major reason for masked bobwhite population declines in the United States and Mexico [65] (see Threats), and livestock have been excluded from the BANWR since its establishment in 1985 [47]. A study of masked bobwhite habitat ecology stated that if grazing is applied, stocking densities should be low, and pasture rested to maintain abundant and diverse native herbaceous vegetation [51]. However, removing livestock grazing without additional management did not improve habitat conditions for masked bobwhites on the refuge [67] (see Fire Effects on Habitats).

Cattle production is the primary livelihood of landowners that manage rangelands in the masked bobwhite’s range in Sonora, and hundreds of thousands of hectares of native desert and thorn scrub vegetation have been converted to buffelgrass pastures for livestock forage production [22,23,43], suggesting that habitat restoration is essential to the recovery of masked bobwhites in Sonora. While livestock grazing may temporarily reduce buffelgrass cover [87], buffelgrass can generally withstand continuous and heavy grazing (e.g., [57]), and ceasing livestock grazing is unlikely to reduce buffelgrass dominance and increase native species without additional control treatments [82]. See the FEIS Species Review about buffelgrass for information about livestock grazing and buffelgrass control.

### Supplementing Water

Northern bobwhites typically fulfill most of their daily water requirements via the foods they consume [65]. However, researchers have suggested that during prolonged drought water be supplemented to increase or maintain food for masked bobwhites [65] and supplemental water is being provided to masked bobwhites at the BANWR [114].

### Management Under a Changing Climate

In Arizona and southern Arizona specifically, mean maximum temperatures are predicted to rise, while monthly precipitation projections are less certain but may remain similar to current levels. Combined, this may result in drier conditions due to an evaporative deficit [98]. While effects of current and anticipated climate change on masked bobwhites are unknown, much of the effect is likely to depend on precipitation amount and timing due to its influence on reproductive success. If summer monsoonal precipitation increases, herbaceous vegetation and leguminous shrubs may increase abundance. Such increases would likely provide conditions needed to trigger and/or prolong breeding activity in masked bobwhites and thereby improve nesting success. However, if winter rainfall is reduced or more sporadic, forb growth in late winter and early spring may be less, reducing food needed to sustain birds until the onset of the monsoon season [90,99]. Furthermore, climate changes that are likely to be detrimental to masked bobwhite populations include an increase in the frequency and/or duration of drought [79,90], those that increase fire frequency and/or size, or increase the establishment and spread of nonnative invasive grasses (e.g., [91,115]) (see Fire Effects on Habitats).
