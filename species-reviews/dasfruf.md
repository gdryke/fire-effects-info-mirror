## Introduction

## Taxonomy

The subspecies of shrubby cinquefoil that occurs in North America is _Dasiphora fruticosa_ (L.) Rydb. subsp. _floribunda_(Pursh) Kartesz (Rosaceae) [60,80,91,185,193]. The typical subspecies, _Dasiphora fruticosa_ (L.) Rydb. subsp. _fruticosa_ , occurs in Europe [59].

## Distribution

## General Distribution

Shrubby cinquefoil is widely distributed throughout the northern hemisphere. It is found in Europe, Asia, and North America [149]. In North America, shrubby cinquefoil ranges from Alaska and the Northwest Territories east through Canada to Labrador, Newfoundland, and Greenland. Shrubby cinquefoil's distribution extends south to California, Arizona, New Mexico, Iowa, Illinois, Indiana, Ohio, West Virginia, Maryland, Delaware, and New Jersey [36,85,149,190,191]. The PLANTS database provides a map of shrubby cinquefoil's distribution in the United States.

## Site Characteristics

Shrubby cinquefoil occupies a wide variety of sites and is distributed from low valleys to mountain peaks [149,183], growing in riparian communities [14,22,28,63,82,114,140,164,183,199], around springs [17,66,67,68,145], wetland sites [14,82,84,116,140], upland sites [6,63,169,195], rock ledges [1,125], and subalpine and alpine sites [8,9,38,123,125]. In Alberta alone, shrubby cinquefoil occurs on low, moist riparian sites, prairies, dry rock ledges, open mountain valleys, and boreal forests where it is confined to swamps and riparian areas [165,191]. In intermountain areas, it is well adapted to wet meadows and subalpine areas [142]. In western Montana, shrubby cinquefoil is likely to occur in mountain stream bottoms; on the east side of the Continental Divide it is more widespread, occurring in large open areas, particularly subalpine meadows [149,179].

Shrubby cinquefoil often occurs as transitional vegetation from wetland sites to drier upland sites [116,200] and from foothills to mountains [85] in the western part of its range. Though present next to rivers, shrubby cinquefoil may contribute more to shrub cover 65 to 130 feet (20-40 m) from riverbanks [136].

Shrubby cinquefoil has fair to weakly moderate drought tolerance in the western United States [190], and has been rated moderate to high drought tolerance in Alberta [191]. Shrubby cinquefoil prefers open sites but will grow under light shade [126,135,183,191]. Though it is moderately shade tolerant, shrubby cinquefoil flowers more abundantly in nearly full sun [190].

### Topography

Shrubby cinquefoil occupies toeslopes, mid-slopes, and upper slopes, as well as old stream terraces of steep-sided valleys [82,114,117,118,132,133]. In the Black Hills of South Dakota, shrubby cinquefoil grows on exposed ridges [178]. Shrubby cinquefoil is also found on gently rolling or sloping topography [17,82,127,169,182], in broad meadows [66,67,68,82,133], and on broad terraces adjacent to streams [14,17,66,67,68,133,150]. It grows on slopes ranging from 1-20% [114,133].

### Elevation

Shrubby cinquefoil grows in the subarctic zone of northern Canada [95], occurring from 4,760 to 5,413 feet (1,450-1,650 m) in the Yukon Territory [13]. It grows up to at least 4,920 feet (1,500 m) in Alaska [80]. In the Rocky Mountain States, shrubby cinquefoil's distribution ranges from the prairie and foothills to the alpine regions. In Montana, it grows from 2,820 to 9,500 feet (859-2895 m) [6,14,66,67,68,104,120,127,129,135,140]; in Idaho, from 4,820 to12,000 feet (1,469-3,658 m) [27,63,82,125]; in Wyoming from 6,500 to 8,600 feet (1,981-2,621 m) [182]; in Utah, from 6,000 to 10,000 feet (1,828-3,048 m) [133]; in Colorado, from 9,000 to 11,000 feet (2743-3353 m) [79]. In New Mexico, it is common in the alpine zone above 11,500 feet (3,505 m) [123]. In Nevada, shrubby cinquefoil grows in the subalpine zone, 8,500-8,800 feet (2,591-2,682 m) [114].

### Precipitation

Shrubby cinquefoil occurs on sites receiving 10-30 inches (254-762 mm) of annual precipitation [20,37,127,129,146,182].

### Soils

Shrubby cinquefoil grows on depositional substrates including alluvial, morainal, glaciofluvial, and colluvial [27,78,82,117,133,145]. Parent materials vary [200] and include limestone, sandstone, granite, and basalt [17,27,85,108,127,146]. Soils may be poorly to well-drained; shrubby cinquefoil is tolerant of wet conditions and flooding, and calcareous substrates [1,27,121,128,151,191].

In the western part of its range, shrubby cinquefoil is found on a wide range of soil classes [190], from clay, fine loam, sandy loam, and loamy skeletal soils [17,27,63,66,67,68,114,133,134,170,191,200] to coarse textured soils [145,178,183,191,199]. It grows well on most textural classes, except dense clay and loose sand [190]. Soils supporting shrubby cinquefoil are moderately deep [27,118,127,182] to very deep [182,200], often with humus layers [118]. Confining rock layers may be present beneath the soil [17]. Shrubby cinquefoil occurs on xerophytic sites [1,134] and on mesic sites [30,106,125,126,169,178,182] with moderate to high estimated water holding capacity that dry out early in the growing season [114,133,200]. In Idaho, shrubby cinquefoil occurs in calcareous fens, phreatophytic woodlands, and spring-fed meadows [82].

In the eastern part of its range, shrubby cinquefoil is found primarily on moist lowland forest sites [25], occurring on dry or wet sites of river banks, lakeshores, fens and marshes [81,83,85,157,158,172]. It occurs on sandy, rocky, and gravelly soils including sand dunes and rock ledges [31,131,172], and on poorly drained soils, occurring in mesotrophic and eutrophic fens [50,155]. Fen sites are often calcareous [21,45,163] with a pH of 5.5-8.0 [163]. Shrubby cinquefoil dominates peatland vegetation in Indiana where pH is 6.9 [177]. Shrubby cinquefoil may dominate the vegetation around calcareous seeps in Illinois where soil is maintained in a saturated condition [176]. In New York, shrubby cinquefoil often occurs on moist marl beds [166]. In Michigan grasslands, shrubby cinquefoil increases in abundance at the lower end of drainage gradients and may dominate vegetation on associated wetland sites [174].

Shrubby cinquefoil is tolerant of weakly saline and moderately acid to moderately basic soils; it is often found on calcareous sites [6,17,20,50,142,145,146,170,178,180,190]. Shrubby cinquefoil is tolerant of poor soil [115], and it has been found on depleted, unproductive sites [33].

## Plant Communities

### Eastern North America

Shrubby cinquefoil is common in fen and meadow vegetation in the eastern part of its range. In Newfoundland, shrubby cinquefoil occurs in fen larch (_Larix_ spp.) forests with bog birch (_Betula glandulosa_), swamp birch (_Betula pumila_), yellow sedge (_Carex pensylvanica_), northern singlespike sedge (_Carex scirpoidea_), sweet gale (_Myrica gale_), and white meadowsweet (_Spiraea alba_ var. _latifolia_) [155], and is an indicator species of rich fens [144]. Shrubby cinquefoil is common in fens and bogs in Maine [158], may dominate peatland vegetation in Indiana [177], and is a typical indicator species of fens in Ohio [171]. Shrubby cinquefoil occurs in bog meadow vegetation in Ohio with ninebark (_Physocarpus malvaceus_), dogwood (_Cornus_ spp.), and swamp birch [57]. It is common in wetland vegetation dominated by sedges (_Carex_ spp.) [98] and is associated with mat muhly (_Muhlenbergia richardsonis_) [45] in Wisconsin. Shrubby cinquefoil occurs as a codominant with tussock sedge (_Carex stricta_), Ohio goldenrod (_Solidago ohioensis_), and big bluestem (_Andropogon gerardii_ var. _gerardii_) in Illinois fens [176].

Shrubby cinquefoil is common in prairie dropseed (_Sporobolus heterolepis_)-little bluestem (_Schizachyrium scoparium_) grasslands [174] and is a common understory species of black oak (_Quercus velutina_) communities on sand dunes [131] in Michigan.

### Western North America

Shrubby cinquefoil is associated with willows (_Salix_ spp.), alders (_Alnus_ spp.), birch (_Betula_ spp.), sedges, rushes (_Juncus_ spp.), and tufted hairgrass (_Deschampsia cespitosa_) [190], and is a common shrub associate of blue spruce (_Picea pungens_) [51] in western North America. It is common in open taiga spruce (_Picea_ spp.) forests [1,95,132]. In northern Rockies peatlands, shrubby cinquefoil occurs in the water sedge (_Carex aquatilis_) community type [23]. In the northern Rockies and the Intermountain West, shrubby cinquefoil is common in mountain brush and quaking aspen (_Populus tremuloides_) communities [128,142,194]. It occurs in the central Rockies in ponderosa pine (_Pinus ponderosa_) forests with Douglas-fir (Pseudotsuga menziesii), limber pine (Pinus flexilis), blue spruce, quaking aspen, cliffbush (_Jamesia americana_), common juniper (_Juniperus communis_), bearberry (_Arctostaphylos uva-ursi_), wax currant (_Ribes cereum_), and Wood's rose (_Rosa woodsii_) [153].

#### Western Canada and Alaska

In northwestern Canada, shrubby cinquefoil is common in the medium shrub/entire leaf mountainavens (_Dryas integrifolia_) plant association and prominent in the white spruce (_Picea glauca_)/shrub/mountainavens plant association [180]. In the Yukon Territory, it occurs with American green alder (_Alnus viridis_ ssp. _crispa_) and prickly rose (_Rosa acicularis_) [13]. In western Canada, shrubby cinquefoil commonly occurs in jack pine (_Pinus banksiana_) forests [24,42]; in the black spruce (_Picea mariana_) forests with bog Labrador tea (_Ledum groenlandicum_), blueberry willow (_Salix myrtillifolia_), and prickly rose [24,42,118]; and in the white spruce-willow-birch plant association, with grayleaf willow (_Salix glauca_), planeleaf willow (_S. planifolia_ ssp. _planifolia_), Scouler willow (_S. scouleriana_), Bebb willow (_S. bebbiana_), bog birch, and russet buffaloberry (_Shepherdia canadensis_) [117,118,143]. It may occur as a shrub dominant in some black spruce and white spruce communities in British Columbia [139]. It is common in the whitebark pine (_Pinus albicaulis_)/common juniper plant association in British Columbia and Alberta, occurring with russet buffaloberry and bearberry [130]; and in the limber pine forests with common juniper, Rocky Mountain juniper (_Juniperus scopulorum_), creeping juniper (_J. horizontalis_), bearberry, prickly rose, common snowberry (_Symphoricarpos albus_), and Saskatoon serviceberry (_Amelanchier alnifolia_) [1]. Shrubby cinquefoil is common in lodgepole pine (_Pinus contorta_) stands [103] and in the prairie Junegrass (_Koeleria macrantha_)-creeping juniper shrub savannah plant association [173] in the Canadian Rockies. It is an important constituent in the grasslands of the Canadian Rockies, occurring in the rough fescue (_Festuca altaica_) grasslands [20,30,110,126,152,196]. It is commonly found in the rough fescue/Parry's danthonia (_Danthonia parryi_) plant association of Alberta [169]. It may codominate grasslands with bearberry and prickly rose in western Canada [173]. Shrubby cinquefoil is common in riparian and forest wetland vegetation [84,92].

Shrubby cinquefoil occurs in the open willow shrub communities of interior Alaska [69,197]. It is a common understory shrub in white spruce stands [56] and in the open black spruce forests of Alaska, occurring with bog blueberry (_Vaccinium uliginosum_), mountain cranberry (_Vaccinium vitis-idaea_), Labrador tea, black crowberry (_Empetrum nigrum_), and red bearberry (_Arctostaphylos rubra_) [10,56]. It may form a dense shrub layer with russet buffaloberry, diamondleaf willow (_Salix planifolia_ ssp. _pulchra_), prickly rose, and saplings of white spruce and balsam poplar (_Populus balsamifera_) in upland taiga vegetation [113]. Shrubby cinquefoil is a common understory species in Alaska willow (_Salix alaxensis_) and American green alder stands [37]. Shrubby cinquefoil is common in freshwater marshes and tall fescue (_Festuca arundinacea_) and bluejoint reedgrass (_Calamagrostis canadensis_) grasslands [69], and may occur as a dominant in arctic alpine plant communities [87].

#### Western United States

Shrubby cinquefoil is a codominant in the montane grasslands of north-central Montana, occurring with Idaho fescue (_Festuca idahoensis_) and rough fescue [111,179]. In some areas shrubby cinquefoil has become established in large continuous stands [149]. It is also a codominant with rough fescue in a tundra-like plant community of subalpine grassland above timberline [179]. It is a common associate of bearberry, rockdwelling sedge (_Carex rupestris_), and russet buffaloberry on subalpine sites [6]. Shrubby cinquefoil commonly occurs with creeping juniper in the juniper (_Juniperus_ spp.)/cinquefoil (_Potentilla_ spp.)/fescue (_Festuca_ spp.) and juniper/cinquefoil/sedgeplant associations. It is also common in juniper/alpineoatgrass (_Helictotrichon_ spp.) and juniper/little bluestem/fescue plant associations [120]. It may also occur with big sagebrush (_Artemisia tridentata_) or silver sagebrush (_Artemisia cana_) in eastern and southwestern Montana [127,129,135,136]; and with willow [140], onespike danthonia (_Danthonia unispicata_), and mountain brome (_Bromus carinatus_) [129] in southwestern Montana. Shrubby cinquefoil also forms a major riparian or wetland vegetation type in Montana [64,66,67,68]. It is common in Douglas-fir and lodgepole pine forests [107] and in the Douglas-fir/common snowberry habitat type [97].

Shrubby cinquefoil occurs sparsely in Rocky Mountain juniper stands [147] and in the wooded draws of upland grass communities [201] in the badlands of North Dakota. It occurs commonly in this region with skunkbush sumac (_Rhus trilobata_) and Saskatoon serviceberry [160,201]. In the Black Hills of South Dakota, shrubby cinquefoil is the most common understory species in the ponderosa pine/shrubby cinquefoil/common snowberry/woodland strawberry (_Fragaria vesca_) plant community [40,178].

In western Wyoming, shrubby cinquefoil occurs in silver sagebrush, shrub swamp communities, and occasionally in riparian and aspen forests [7,100,150,199]. It is common in big sagebrush/bitterbrush (_Purshia tridentata_) [78] and big sagebrush/Sandberg bluegrass (_Poa secunda_) [170] plant associations. Shrubby cinquefoil is a characteristic shrub of the spruce (_Picea_ spp.)-fir (_Abies_ spp.) zone [26]. It is commonly found with Englemann spruce (_Picea engelmannii_), blue spruce, lodgepole pine, subalpine fir (_Abies lasiocarpa_), narrowleaf cottonwood (_Populus angustifolia_), common snowberry, pachistima (_Pachistima myrsinites_), red-osier dogwood (_Cornus sericea_), common juniper, Oregon-grape (_Mahonia repens_), russet buffaloberry [199], big sagebrush, silver sagebrush [200], timber oatgrass (_Danthonia intermedia_) [182], tufted hairgrass, Wolf's trisetum (_Trisetum wolfii_), alpine timothy (_Phleum alpinum_), varileaf cinquefoil (_Potentilla diversifolia_), and meadow thistle (_Cirsium scariosum_) [116]. Shrubby cinquefoil is common in limber pine habitat types in Wyoming, Montana, and Idaho [15,55].

In Idaho wetland and riparian communities, shrubby cinquefoil commonly occurs with quaking aspen, sedges, and willows [27,28,145]. The shrubby cinquefoil/timber oatgrass plant association forms riparian complexes with sedge and willow communities [29].

In Nevada, shrubby cinquefoil commonly occurs with Gray's licoriceroot (_Ligusticum grayii_), slender wheatgrass (_Elymus trachycaulus_), rosy pussytoes (_Antennaria microphylla_), northwest cinquefoil (_Potentilla gracilis_), California false hellebore (_Veratrum californicum_), and Fendler's meadowrue (_Thalictrum fendleri_) [114].

In Utah, shrubby cinquefoil commonly occurs with silver sagebrush or mountain snowberry (_Symphoricarpos oreophilus_) and bog birch, Wolf's willow (_Salix wolfii_), smallwing sedge (_Carex microptera_), clustered field sedge (_C. praegracilis_), and Baltic rush (_Juncus balticus_) [133,200]. It is also common in quaking aspen stands [90], lodgepole pine forests [133], and with narrowleaf cottonwood, common juniper, and bearberry [134]. Shrubby cinquefoil occurs as a prominent shrub layer associate in the spruce-fir zone of Utah [72].

Shrubby cinquefoil is also common in the spruce-fir zone of Colorado [79], and is a common understory species in aspen stands, ponderosa pine, and lodgepole pine forests, occurring with Engelmann spruce, subalpine fir, Douglas-fir, rose (_Rosa_ spp.), russet buffaloberry, and common juniper [32,33,106].

In New Mexico, shrubby cinquefoil commonly occurs in alpine tundra vegetation [123] and in spruce-fir forests with quaking aspen, Engelmann spruce, Douglas-fir, and ponderosa pine [33,137].

## Botanical and Ecological Characteristics

## Botanical Description

Shrubby cinquefoil is native to North America, Asia, and Europe [5,46,190] and is cold tolerant and winter hardy [190]. It is a deciduous, multi-stemmed [149] and many branched shrub [47,172,183,191] reaching heights of 1 to 6.5 feet (0.3-2 m) [47,93,104,172,183,190,191], but occurring as a cushion plant in alpine areas [9]. Shrubby cinquefoil branches are pubescent in the 1st year, becoming brown and glabrous in the 2nd year [47,104,172]. Bark becomes fibrous on branches in the 2nd and 3rd years [47,93,149,172,190]. Mature plants have both erect and prostrate branches [47,80,190], the latter able to root adventitiously [47]. The growth form of shrubby cinquefoil varies; it occurs as a low mat and as an erect shrub [126,149,191].

Shrubby cinquefoil has numerous leaves with 3 to 9 leaflets that have long white hairs on both surfaces [47,80]. The lower surface of the leaves has many stomata; the upper surface has none [47].

Shrubby cinquefoil flowers are terminal, occasionally with solitary flowers but usually with many flowers in close clusters [47,104,172]. Varieties of shrubby cinquefoil from North America are monoecious [46,47]. In pollination experiments, shrubby cinquefoil from North America was found self-incompatible [34]. Shrubby cinquefoil produces a compact head of hairy achenes that may persist in winter months [104,172,190].

Shrubby cinquefoil has a shallow to moderately deep, spreading root system [149,190] with thin woody roots [47].

Shrubby cinquefoil is a "long-lived" perennial [191]; on moist marl sites in New York, the average age of shrubby cinquefoil plants was 25.3 years, with the oldest plant 36 years old [166].

## Seasonal Development

In North America, shrubby cinquefoil resumes growth in early spring to early summer [173,190]. Shrubby cinquefoil flowers from late May to late September [57,172,190]. The main flowering of shrubby cinquefoil occurs in July on older growth and in August on current year growth [165]. Seed maturation occurs in late summer to early fall [172,190].

## Regeneration Processes

Shrubby cinquefoil regenerates from wind-dispersed seed and by sprouting from the root crown [39,47,74,149,190,191]. The number of flowers on each plant varies, so seed production is also variable among individuals [47]. Generally, shrubby cinquefoil produces approximately 50 seeds per flower [165]. Seed undergoes a dormancy period; there is no evidence that frost is required to break dormancy [47]. In laboratory experiments, seed germination rates range from 55% to 82%. Growth is slow and 5 years may be required for full stands to develop [190].

Though the normal means of reproduction is through seed, shrubby cinquefoil also spreads vegetatively from adventitious rooting of prostrate stems [47,149]. Elkington and Woodell [47] note that on some sites, particularly those periodically inundated, this type of vegetative reproduction may be highly effective.

Shrubby cinquefoil grows well under greenhouse conditions from both seed and cuttings [39]. It is easily propagated from softwood cuttings and establishes well from rooted cuttings [43,105]. Shrubby cinquefoil transplants readily from the wild and from bare-root or container nursery stock. Seeding sites must be moist for good establishment; shrubby cinquefoil seedlings are durable and persistent once established [183,190].

## Successional Status

Shrubby cinquefoil occurs as both a dominant species and a minor understory species in a variety of vegetation types and occurs in both early and late seral stages. Shrubby cinquefoil occurs on forest edges and in openings created by disturbance [87]. It colonizes disturbed sites [85,96,146] and may be vigorous due to reduced competition from canopy species [85]. Shrubby cinquefoil occurs as a dominant in vegetation on dry, disturbed sites in Alaska [38]. Shrubby cinquefoil is common in both slowly and rapidly progressing successional vegetation of riparian areas and persists through the successional stages [37]. Shrubby cinquefoil may occur as part of climax or subclimax vegetation on seral floodplains in Wyoming [78]. In Alaskan taiga vegetation, shrubby cinquefoil occurs on surfaces disturbed annually by flooding, ice scouring and sediment deposition/erosion. On these sites, shrubby cinquefoil forms a dense shrub layer with russet buffaloberry, prickly rose, willow, and white spruce and balsam poplar saplings [113]. The presence of shrubby cinquefoil in big sagebrush/Sandberg bluegrass communities in Wyoming may indicate an unstable plant community, seral to a subalpine climax forest [170].

Shrubby cinquefoil may be an indicator of climax vegetation on moderately moist Montana grassland and shrub foothill communities east of the Continental Divide [127]. It is a major shrub component on undisturbed sites in fescue grassland [126]. In the shrubby cinquefoil/tufted hairgrass habitat type, canopy cover of shrubby cinquefoil averages 20-29% [14,63,68], and may form pure stands with more than 7500 plants per acre on sites in Montana [93]. In western Wyoming, stands of shrubby cinquefoil/tufted hairgrass may be mid-seral, while stands of shrubby cinquefoil/Idaho fescue are relatively stable [200].

## Fire Ecology and Management

## Immediate Fire Effects

Shrubby cinquefoil is susceptible to fire top-kill [7,15,16,99,129,190], but may survive low- to moderate-severity fires [15,16,190] and resprout vigorously [149]. Fischer and Clayton [55] found shrubby cinquefoil susceptible to fire in eastern Montana, and Kessell and Potter [97] found that even a low-intensity fire removed shrubby cinquefoil from Douglas-fir/common snowberry habitat types in Montana.

Research suggests that spring burns are less damaging to shrubby cinquefoil and cause less mortality than do summer or fall burns [190].

Though spring burns are reported to be less damaging, research results are conflicting. A spring burn in central Montana resulted in little mortality of shrubby cinquefoil because most plants readily resprouted [93]. Wet or saturated soil moisture levels on these sites were high enough to prevent damage to the root crown. Nimir and Payne [129] found higher rates of mortality and little resprouting on another spring burn in Montana; shrubby cinquefoil was clearly damaged by fire on these sites.

## Postfire Regeneration Strategy

 * Small shrub, adventitious bud/root crown
 * Secondary colonizer - off-site seed [175]

## Fire Adaptations

Shrubby cinquefoil is susceptible to damage by fire [7,15,16,99,129,190]; however, if the root crown remains undamaged individual plants readily resprout [14,15,16,66,67,93,127,129,132,182,190]. Shrubby cinquefoil also re-establishes from off-site seed sources [47,190].

The fibrous bark of shrubby cinquefoil results in very flashing, high intensity fuel [93,149]. The high density of shrubby cinquefoil growing in pure stands (up to 7,500 plants/acre or 18,750 plants/ha) may increase fire spread and intensity; when growing in conjunction with shrubby cinquefoil, bunchgrasses may contribute to shrubby cinquefoil's flammability [93].

Fire occurs infrequently on many shrubby cinquefoil sites; some areas are too moist to carry fire [190]. Other sites, such as talus slopes, may lack sufficient fuels to carry fire [93].

## Plant Response to Fire

Following fire, shrubby cinquefoil readily resprouts from the surviving root crown [14,15,16,66,67,93,127,129,132,182,190]. When the root crown is damaged by fire, re-establishment occurs from off-site seed [47]. If resprouting occurs, recovery of shrubby cinquefoil is relatively rapid, but seedling growth is slower and full stand development may take up to 5 years [190].

## Fire Management Considerations

Due to the ability of shrubby cinquefoil to readily resprout, burning to reduce shrubby cinquefoil density is normally ineffective [14,63,66,68,149]. Percent cover of shrubby cinquefoil remained at preburn levels in response to a relatively "cool" prescribed burn in Maine [157,158]. However, burning in sagebrush vegetation in southwestern Montana significantly reduced (p<0.01) basal cover of shrubby cinquefoil [129], and percent cover has also been reduced by fire in Illinois fens [176].

The fibrous, highly flammable bark of shrubby cinquefoil may result in high intensity prescribed burns in stands of shrubby cinquefoil. In one study in central Montana, defoliation of overstory conifers was common due to high intensity burning of shrubby cinquefoil in the understory, resulting in 90% mortality of trees [93].

On one southwestern Montana site, browsing of shrubby cinquefoil after prescribed burning was substantially greater than browsing prior to burning [94].

For more information about fire effects and management considerations of shrubby cinquefoil, see the "Fire Case Studies" section of this FEIS summary.

## Importance to Wildlife and Livestock

Though it has low forage value [120], shrubby cinquefoil's widespread distribution, persistent leaves, and low spreading growth form make it an important source of forage for ungulates [14,35,67,183].

Sites abundant in shrubby cinquefoil in New Mexico generally have a low potential for grazing [33]. Shrubby cinquefoil is grazed in some areas by domestic goats, sheep, and cattle [93,126,149,191,195].

Though it is not preferred forage for deer in Utah [5], shrubby cinquefoil is lightly browsed by mule deer, elk, mountain goats, and bighorn sheep throughout its range [56,102,126,149,162,191]. Winter use of shrubby cinquefoil by deer and elk is also typically light [14,67]. It is a low preference shrub for bighorn sheep, though it receives moderate to heavy use when new growth begins [173]. Shrubby cinquefoil stems are important elk winter browse in Colorado [77].

Small birds and mammals consume shrubby cinquefoil seeds [190].

## Value for Rehabilitation of Disturbed Sites

![Flowering shrubby cinquefoil growing on a scree slope on a steep mountainside below a trail. Conifers dominate below, and rocky mountains extend into the distance.]({{ site.baseurl }}/assets/images/species-reviews/feis-dasfruf_mountain.jpg)

Photo by Eric William Bartram, iNaturalist.org, some rights reserved. 

Shrubby cinquefoil growing on an unstable slope ([CC BY-NC 4.0](<https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nc/4.0/>)).

Shrubby cinquefoil has been found to successfully revegetate disturbed lands [54,112,142,183]. It establishes poorly from seed but very well if transplanted [142]. One study of nursery stock survival on disturbed sites in Alaska found 97% survival of seedlings after 2 years and 100% survival of rooted cuttings after 1 year. Planting nursery stock also provides visual amelioration on disturbed sites [39]. Five years or more may be required for full stands to develop from seed [190]. Shrubby cinquefoil has been rated high for biomass production, moderate for erosion control potential, low to moderate for short-term revegetation potential, and moderate to high for long-term revegetation potential [14,41,63,66,67,68].

Shrubby cinquefoil sometimes occurs as a dominant plant species on dry, unstable cut slopes and is recommended for revegetating dry, disturbed sites [38,54] and roadsides [183,191]. It colonizes oil spills [96], seismic lines [95], and abandoned coal mining sites [159] in Canada. Shrubby cinquefoil is successful in revegetating mining-disturbed lands [18,191] and has shown good potential for growth and survival on amended mine tailings [190], achieving a 67-100% survival rate [52,53]. Because shrubby cinquefoil readily establishes from nursery grown stock, grows quickly, and provides excellent soil stability, it is well adapted for revegetating disturbed streambank and moist meadow sites [14,63,66,67,68].

## Other Uses

On northern and western grasslands, shrubby cinquefoil commonly occurs with rough fescue, which may benefit from the protection afforded by shrubby cinquefoil. Rough fescue may experience increased vigor as a result, and on some sites may fail to set seed without the presence of shrubby cinquefoil [111].

Shrubby cinquefoil is useful for erosion control and soil stabilization [47,190,191] and may be useful in wildlife habitat mitigation [61].

Due to its virtually continuous summer blooming, shrubby cinquefoil is a valuable landscape ornamental [34,38,47,183,190,191], and is recommended for landscaping in deer winter range areas because it is seldom browsed [5].

Native Americans use dried leaves of shrubby cinquefoil to make tea. Dried leaves can also be made into a mixture considered an arrow poison that was thought to go directly to the heart. Dried, finely powdered leaves were used to protect the body from severe, temporary heat [71].

## Other Management Considerations

Canopy cover of shrubby cinquefoil increases in response to moderate grazing, while heavy grazing decreases canopy cover. Shrubby cinquefoil is tolerant to moderate defoliation by grazing but is injured by repeated defoliation, especially when accompanied by drought conditions [14,63,66,67,190,191]. Shrubby cinquefoil may be killed by browsing [21,48] or trampling; in a Wisconsin study, significantly fewer (p<0.05) shrubby cinquefoil plants were found on or near animal trails than in the surrounding vegetation [21]. On study sites in New York, invasion by shrubby cinquefoil corresponds to the cessation of grazing activities [50].

The spread of continuous stands of shrubby cinquefoil in western grasslands may be associated with excessive grazing [127,149,165,182]. On bighorn sheep range in western Canada, shrubby cinquefoil was positively correlated with total forage utilization and stocking rates but negatively correlated with forage production. The proportion of shrubby cinquefoil in the vegetation increased as forage utilization increased and the stocking rate increased, but the proportion decreased as forage production/ha increased. Study results indicated that in using shrubby cinquefoil as an index to range condition, grasslands containing more than 5% shrubby cinquefoil, and especially those containing more than 10%, were overgrazed [173]. However, another study in fescue grassland of western Canada found no significant difference in shrubby cinquefoil percent cover at different stocking rates, which ranged from low to very high [196], and other authors report that extensive grazing may substantially decrease the density of shrubby cinquefoil [164,195].

Shrubby cinquefoil may limit forage production on some high elevation grasslands. The density of shrubby cinquefoil has been negatively correlated with forage production [126], and continuous stands of shrubby cinquefoil in particular may limit production and availability of herbaceous forage [149].

Effective control of shrubby cinquefoil has been achieved with herbicide treatments of 2,4-D, 2,4-DE/dichlorprop E, and hexazinone. Little regrowth occurred after treatment, and shrubby cinquefoil control resulted in increased forage production and utilization [126]. Picloram is also effective in controlling shrubby cinquefoil, with higher concentrations causing greater mortality [165].
