# Fire regimes of East Cascades ponderosa pine and montane mixed-conifer communities

## Summary

This Fire Regime Synthesis details characteristics of presettlement (i.e., before Euro-American settlement), historical (i.e., any past record), and contemporary (i.e., 1980s to present) fuels and fire regimes in ponderosa pine and montane mixed-conifer communities in the East Cascade Mountains in the northwestern United States. Stand structure and species composition varies within and among these communities due to variation in site characteristics, climate, disturbance history, and successional stage. Although historical landscapes were successionally diverse, they were characterized by a large proportion of low-density, open-canopy forest dominated by large, old, fire- and drought-tolerant trees (mostly ponderosa pine), with small areas of higher-density forest, and small openings without trees. These stand structures are compatible with a presettlement fire regime dominated by frequent, low- and mixed-severity fires, with high-severity fire effects mostly limited to relatively small (<100-200 ha) patches. This fire regime maintained low fuel biomass, dominated by flammable, often continuous surface fuels, with few ladder fuels, and relatively widely spaced crown fuels. Our analyses found presettlement mean fire interval (MFI) averaged 12 years with a maximum MFI of 24 years in the ponderosa pine forest zone, and MFI averaged 20 years with a maximum MFI of 100 years in the montane mixed-conifer forest zone. Historically, lightning-caused fires could ignite as early as May and as late as October, although fire-scar analyses across all forest types suggest a dominance of late growing season and dormant season fires, with some variability among studies. Lightning ignitions were most common, although American Indians also ignited fires, within and outside lightning season. Evidence suggests that most presettlement fires were small (<400 ha), and larger fires were infrequent. Large fires and widespread fire years were most frequent during relatively warm, dry summers at both site and regional scales. Wetter than average antecedent conditions may be related to large fires on the driest ponderosa pine sites.

Stand structure, species composition, fuels, and fire regime characteristics in contemporary stands differ from presettlement stands to the degree that some consider open-canopy forests dominated by large fire- and drought-tolerant trees to be endangered. Dense forest conditions, once-rare, have become common, whereas open-canopy conditions and large, old trees, once common, are rare. Fire exclusion began during Euro-American settlement, first by reducing ignitions, then by reducing fine fuel biomass and connectivity with livestock grazing, and eventually, by engaging in active fire suppression. Fire exclusion led to increased fuel biomass and presence of ladder fuels. Subsequently, contemporary fire regimes are characterized by increasing frequency of large, high-severity fires, due to both increased fuel biomass and the effects of anthropogenic climate change. Despite contemporary increases in fire frequency and severity, there is an overall fire deficit compared to presettlement fire frequency and area burned by low-severity fire.

Hazardous fuel reduction to reduce fire spread and severity is important for protecting infrastructure in the Wildland Urban Interface (WUI) and the few remaining stands of old and large-diameter dry forests in the East Cascades and elsewhere in the West. Landscapes managed to mimic presettlement fire regimes, forest structure, composition, and fuels are likely to be most resilient to future fires and other disturbances. Wildfire and prescribed fire are important tools for achieving these conditions**.** The effects of contemporary climate and projected climate changes on fire behavior and fire effects must be considered when managing with fire.

## Introduction

Quantifying the components of an ecosystem’s fire regime and how they vary with environmental factors can improve ecologically based fire management decisions. Individual studies provide detailed information on fire regime parameters, but their scope of inference is constrained by site-level heterogeneity and variation in sampling methodologies. The increasing number of published studies that address fire regime characteristics creates a challenge for land managers to efficiently acquire and use the best available science, but it provides an opportunity for analyses and synthesis of this information. The objective of this Fire Regime Synthesis was to systematically locate, review, and synthesize information on historical fire regimes of communities in the East Cascades where ponderosa pine was historically dominant. The ponderosa pine series is widely distributed throughout the East Cascades, and ponderosa pine is also a common seral dominant on cooler, moister sites in the Douglas-fir, white fir, and grand fir series [8].

This synthesis brings together information from the scientific literature (as of 2023) and the Biophysical Settings (BpS) models and associated Fire Regime Data Products developed by LANDFIRE (see LANDFIRE Biophysical Settings section) to summarize what is known about historical and contemporary fire regimes in ponderosa pine and montane mixed-conifer communities in the East Cascades, and it identifies locations and topics where fire history data is lacking. This information supplements that provided by FEIS Species Reviews and can assist with revisions to LANDFIRE models.

Common names are used throughout this synthesis. For a complete list of common and scientific names of plant species mentioned in this synthesis see table A1.

## Methods

### Literature Search and Selection

We searched the scientific literature (using the Citation Retrieval System of the U.S. Forest Service Fire Effects Information System, JSTOR, and Web of Science) for published and unpublished studies using combinations of the following keywords: fire history, fire regime, ponderosa pine, Jeffrey pine, Douglas-fir, grand fir, white fir, Cascade Range, Washington, Oregon, and California. This search process concluded in May 2017. We also searched the literature cited of relevant studies to find additional related papers and continued to find relevant work up until 2023. While a few of these more recent publications were referenced in discussion portions of this review (e.g., [40,111]), data from those studies were not included in any analyses. Papers were reviewed and included in analyses if they met the following criteria: research occurred in the East Cascades of Washington, Oregon, or California in ecosystems dominated by ponderosa or Jeffrey pine, and studies used dendrochronological information from living and dead trees to characterize fire regime parameters such as fire frequency, season, severity, size, and climate relationships. We included both peer-reviewed and gray literature, as long as they provided numerical results based on field observations and were not based on modeling, speculation, or opinion. We identified 25 papers that met these criteria. These provided fire history information for 91 sites.

Using study site and plant community descriptions, we matched sites in these studies to one of the relevant BpS mapped by LANDFIRE and grouped them as either ponderosa pine or mixed-conifer types for analysis. We excluded studies on sites in the Northern Rocky Mountains of northeastern Washington, the Blue Mountains of northeastern Oregon, and the Sierra Nevada of California because these are covered in other FEIS Fire Regime Syntheses, and we excluded sites west of the Cascade crest where the climate has a stronger maritime influence and generally more precipitation. We included a study from the Thousand Lakes Wilderness in the southern Cascades of California [27,28], where the Cascade crest is less distinct, and study sites likely occurred on both east and west sides. These study sites may have more of a westside climatic influence than other sites included in this synthesis [156].

Information about historical and contemporary stand structure, fire ignitions, and fire-climate relationships was not consistently available in fire history studies (table 1). However, related studies and literature reviews found in our search were used to summarize available information on these topics. We used studies that provided information on contemporary stand structures and fires (since the 1980s) to evaluate and discuss changes in fuels and fire regime characteristics (see Contemporary Fuels and Fire Regimes).

### Analyses

To characterize presettlement and historical fire regimes, we extracted site-specific data from studies describing fire seasonality (6 studies), frequency (25 studies), severity (3 studies), pattern and size (7 studies), and fire-climate relationships (3 studies) (table 1) in order to describe the central tendency, range, and variability in the data reported for these sites and make comparisons among time periods when possible. Methods for contemporary fires are straightforward because we can make direct observations and measurements (e.g., using Monitoring Trends in Burn Severity). We provide a review of the literature that examines contemporary fire parameters in these ecosystems, some of which also estimate and provide comparisons to presettlement parameters for the same study area.

Time periods covered varied among the fire history studies examined in this synthesis. Fire-scar records dated back to as early as 1255 [88], but most frequently to the 1600s [16,30,32,52,148,157,168,178]. Authors used different dates to define presettlement, settlement, and postsettlement (also called fire suppression or fire exclusion) periods. In the East Cascades, Euro-American settlement, along with increasingly widespread livestock grazing, began in the 1850s and 1860s [28,47,52,170], leading to changes in land use and fuel characteristics in many areas. Active fire suppression began in the area between 1905 and 1910, although suppression efforts were not widely effective until mid-century, when more advanced equipment and techniques were available (e.g., [52]).

In this synthesis, “presettlement” generally refers to the time before the effects of Euro-American settlement (i.e., fire exclusion) were evident in wildlands, and mostly includes the past 400 to 600 years for which fire-scar records are available. The term “historical” refers to any past record of fires, up until the late 20th century. The term “contemporary” refers to fires in the late 20th (1980s and 1990s) and early 21st centuries, when satellite imagery became available and more widely used for fire analyses. Three fire history studies did not distinguish presettlement from later time periods and instead reported metrics for the fire record as a whole [88,157,187], and the time period was not identified for two data sets [105,106]. To evaluate historical fire activity over past millennia, paleoecological studies that examined charcoal in lake sediments were also included in this synthesis (see Fire Frequency by Time Period).

#### Stand Structure and Fuel Characteristics

The fire history studies reviewed for this synthesis provide little information on historical stand structure and fuel characteristics, other than what may be inferred from estimated fire regime characteristics. Several of these studies [49,128,149,168,170,187] included estimates of historical stand structures for their study area in order to assess contemporary changes induced by fire exclusion (e.g., [168,170]). However, this information is insufficient for characterizing the historical range of variation (HRV) at a landscape-level and represents only a portion of the literature available on historical stand structure in East Cascades ponderosa pine and montane mixed-conifer communities. We did not do an exhaustive search for this literature, but instead summarized information and data from several stand reconstruction studies (see below) in conjunction with relevant literature reviews [7,8,61,71,74,134,166] that assess historical and contemporary stand structure and fuel characteristics for these communities. Methods used for historical stand reconstruction in these studies include those based on historical fire frequency and severity (e.g., [9]), abundance and distribution of existing trees and stumps (e.g., [63,112]), historical timber inventories (e.g., [59,60]), General Land Office (GLO) survey data (e.g., [19,20,187]), and tree-ring analyses (e.g., [119,189]). These reconstructions provide estimates of the characteristic historical abundance of seral stages by potential vegetation type (i.e., HRV).

Comparison of contemporary relative abundance of seral stages to historical estimates can indicate departure from an HRV. For example, fire regime condition class (FRCC) is used to assess ecological departure from HRV [151] by comparing the current proportion of each of five seral stages to its estimated historical abundance: early seral, mid-seral closed canopy, mid-seral open canopy, late seral open canopy, and late seral closed canopy [40,66]. Departure from HRV is assumed to indicate areas less resilient and sustainable than those within the range [40].

#### Fire Season

Six of the fire history studies included in our analysis recorded the position of fire scars within annual growth rings to examine patterns of fire seasonality [16,28,32,123,168,187]. The studies varied in the number of seasonal categories included, ranging from two (earlywood and latewood) to five (early, middle, and late earlywood, latewood, and dormant). To align these results for comparison, we collapsed all earlywood observations into one category, and combined latewood and dormant observations to calculate the mean of early season (spring and early summer) versus late season (late summer to winter) fires. Although it is difficult to attach calendar dates to cambial phenology due to interspecific, intraspecific, and interannual variations, earlywood formation in ponderosa pine trees begins around mid-June and latewood formation begins in late August in the East Cascades of Washington [187] and California [168]. Dormant season scars are interpreted to be from fires in late summer or fall (beginning in mid- to late September) in this region [7,168,187].

#### Ignition Sources

The relative importance of lightning versus humans as historical ignition sources is a topic of interest due to its effects on historical fire seasonality and frequency. However, it is not possible to quantify, due to insufficient data. Rather than quantifying data, we reviewed and summarized qualitative information presented in fire histories and related literature reviews.

#### Fire Frequency

Historical fire frequency data were gathered from 25 studies, in which mean or median fire intervals were calculated for a total of 91 sites in ponderosa pine and montane mixed-conifer communities dispersed throughout the East Cascades from northern Washington to northern California (fig. 3, table A2). In these studies, fire years were determined using fire scars recorded during the past 400 to 600 years, sometimes in combination with cohort initiation dates and/or changes in radial growth. Mean or median fire intervals for the longest period recorded on each study site were used to calculate the mean and range of historical fire intervals by forest zone and plant community type (i.e., estimated BpS), and to examine patterns related to elevation and aspect. Most studies reported mean fire intervals (MFI), although two reported medians [28,168]. 

Most studies reported composite MFIs using fire dates recorded on at least two trees in the study area, while several studies reported only point MFIs [106,108,154,157], including three studies that reported point MFIs from only one tree [88,178,179]. Both metrics were used in our analysis, although for studies that reported both [28,89,124,148,168], we used only the composite in our analysis.

It is important to note that although area size is an important variable for interpreting fire intervals, few studies reported the size of the areas to which the calculated means applied. For those that reported means or medians at multiple scales [30,47,124,148,168], we chose composite means or medians from the smallest area reported to minimize differences in the size of sampled areas. Because many MFIs were point estimates (the smallest scale possible), this approach makes these data most spatially compatible with one another.

The MFIs from Lava Beds National Monument that we included in our analysis [105,106] were lacking information on site characteristics or methods used.

Because information on site characteristics, spatial scales, and comparisons of fire intervals among different historical time periods was not consistently provided in the fire history studies that we examined, we were not able to quantitatively evaluate the effects of these variables on fire interval estimates. Instead, we provide a review of the studies that analyzed the effects of site characteristics, spatial scales, and historical time periods on fire frequency estimates within their study areas.

We also summarized fire frequency information spanning the Holocene, as revealed by analyses of lake sediments from ponderosa pine and mixed-conifer sites in the East Cascades (table A3).

#### Fire Severity

Only two fire history studies provided quantitative estimates of the relative proportions of low-, moderate-, and high-severity fires at a given site [28,170]. However, they all found fire scars, which were used to identify years in which fires occurred and indicate a predominance of low- to moderate-severity fires in the historical record. Several studies also identified patches of historical high-severity fires by cohort initiation dates [47,62,73,77,111,187]. Given the lack of comparable data on fire severity, we review the results of these studies, along with information from BpS succession modeling, other related studies, and relevant literature reviews to describe and compare possible patterns of historical fire severity.

Proportions of area burned at high, moderate, and low severity in a given fire or a given year can be derived for contemporary fires, but this information is difficult to estimate and therefore generally unavailable for historical fires.

#### Fire Size

Historical fire size and pattern are difficult to estimate, and few fire history studies addressed these topics. Those that did used varied approaches to quantify or describe the mean historical fire size, the size-class distribution of historical fires (i.e., frequency of small vs. large fires), the annual area burned, and/or the frequency of widespread or large fire years at a given site. Because the methodology used and scales sampled differed substantially among studies, we were not able to derive meaningful descriptive statistics from these data, and instead provide a review of that literature with a discussion and comparison of their results.

The sizes of all individual fires must be known in order to quantify the mean historical fire size or the size-class distribution of historical fires. To estimate the size of a given fire, researchers used mapped locations of fire evidence (fire-scarred trees, recruitment pulses, and abrupt radial growth changes) to delineate fire perimeters, with upslope boundaries delineated by barriers to fire spread (e.g., lava flows, perennial streams, changes in fuel beds), unless evidence of fire was recorded on both sides of the barrier [28,30,47,148,168,170,187]. Because fire-size estimates include only the portion within sampled study areas, and fires likely burned beyond study area boundaries, actual fire sizes were probably larger than those reported [111,148,168]. Therefore, these estimates might be considered minimum fire sizes.

To determine the occurrence and frequency of widespread fire years, studies that included estimates of individual fire sizes sometimes reported the frequency of fires larger than some minimum size (e.g., [28,168,187]), while others included estimates of annual area burned, along with the frequency of years in which area burned exceeded various thresholds (e.g., [62,111]). Detection criteria included minimum number or percentage of trees scarred or of plots or sites that had evidence of fire in a given year (e.g., [16,30,47,52,123,128,148,168]). For example, Everett et al. (2000) [47] identified “large” fires as those recorded in at least 25% of sampled areas and “major” fires as those recorded in at least 50% of sampled areas, and Norman and Taylor (2003) [123] considered fires “widespread” when they burned at least 7 of 8 sampling units. These criteria could provide evidence of large, continuous fires, or smaller, separate fires that resulted from multiple ignitions; validation with known fire perimeters suggests the former [62]. Either way, they likely indicate years in which the total area burned was above average, with large fires accounting for most of the area burned in a given year (e.g., [1,47]). In this review these are hereafter referred to as widespread fire years. Some studies also included comparisons of widespread fire years to climate reconstructions to look for relationships (e.g., [123,187]).

#### Fire-Climate Relationships

We evaluated the relationship between climate and historical widespread fire years in the East Cascades by selecting studies that performed superposed epoch analysis (SEA) on the association between fire year and reconstructed climatic indices [32,75,123,136,169,187]. These fire history studies used SEA to discern possible patterns in climatic indices (including the Palmer Drought Severity Index (PDSI), Southern Oscillation Index (SOI), and Pacific Decadal Oscillation (PDO)) during and preceding fire years.

Historical widespread fire years in the Cascade Range were determined from site-based fire history data, and studies used different criteria to designate widespread fire years (see Analyses: Fire Size).

## Distribution

## General Distribution

The ponderosa pine and mixed-conifer communities covered in this synthesis occur on the eastern slopes of the Cascade Range from Lassen Peak in California to the Canadian border in Washington (fig. 2), at elevations ranging from foothill to montane (600–2,100 m) [51,53]. The climate is mostly continental with some areas of maritime influence and is characterized by hot, dry summers; cold, wet winters; and wide diurnal temperature fluctuations in summer [8,53,156].

Due to the rain shadow from the Cascade and Coast ranges, available moisture in East Cascade forest zones is dramatically less than in forests with maritime climates in the western Cascades and along the Cascade crest [8,53,156]. Mean annual precipitation reported at weather stations in the East Cascades ranged from 360 to 1,160 mm in Washington and Oregon [8,53] and from 300 to 1,050 mm in northern California [156,168]. Mean annual precipitation ranged from 380 to 1,870 mm from the most xeric ponderosa pine to the wettest mixed-conifer communities on study sites in the Oregon East Cascades [59,60]. Most precipitation falls from late fall to early spring (75% from November to April), mainly as snow [8], and very little precipitation falls during the growing season.

Wind patterns have substantial impacts on fire occurrence and behavior in eastside forests. For example, warm, dry foehn or Chinook winds are common and occur several times a month during the summer and fall. These winds can quickly dry the fine fuels that carry fire and can also be strong enough to encourage rapid fire spread. Valleys that trend east-west, with low saddles at the crests, are more often affected by these winds than are north-south valleys or areas with more topographic relief. In major drainages that flow from the Cascade crest to the east, down-canyon afternoon winds can also significantly affect fire behavior [8].

## Plant Communities

Plant community composition in the East Cascades responds to moisture and temperature gradients, such that plant communities generally occur in elevational zones, although this relationship is modified by complex topography. The communities covered in this synthesis occur in the ponderosa pine, Douglas-fir, and grand fir zones in the East Cascades of Washington and Oregon [53] and at low to middle elevations on the eastern side of the southern Cascade Range of California [51].

The following summaries describe historical or potential vegetation and are based mostly on general descriptions of vegetation zones by Franklin and Dyrness (1988) [53] and Fites-Kaufmann et al. (2007) [51] in addition to ecological system descriptions from NatureServe [121]. The corresponding numbers of BpS series mapped and modeled by LANDFIRE [93] are shown in parentheses after plant community group names.

### East Cascades Ponderosa Pine

Plant community composition and stand structure in the ponderosa pine zone of the East Cascades varies depending largely on moisture availability and fire history. Ponderosa pine is typically the only dominant tree species throughout most of the zone; however, in the southern Cascade Range, communities in the ponderosa pine zone may be dominated by either ponderosa pine, Jeffrey pine, or both.

#### Ponderosa Pine Forest, Woodland, and Savanna (10531, 10532, 11650)

These communities occur in a narrow band (about 15–30 km wide) along the foothills of the eastern slope of the Cascade Range in Oregon and Washington, and on the pumice plateau of south-central Oregon. They generally occur at lower elevations in the northern part of their distribution (e.g., between 600 and 1,200 m in Washington) and at higher elevations in the southern part (e.g., between 1,450 and 2,000 m in south-central Oregon) [8,53]. They occur on all slopes and aspects; however, they are common on warm, dry, exposed sites on moderately steep to very steep slopes or ridgetops [121].

Columbia ponderosa pine is dominant, and Douglas-fir may be present. The shrub layer varies from dense to sparse and may include big sagebrush, greenleaf manzanita, kinnikinnick, mallow ninebark, curlleaf mountain-mahogany, antelope bitterbrush, chokecherry, Saskatoon serviceberry, and/or snowberry. The herbaceous layer is typically dominated by pinegrass or Geyer’s sedge on mesic sites and by bluebunch wheatgrass, Idaho fescue, needle and thread, needlegrass, and/or long-stolon sedge on xeric sites [121]. Ponderosa pine communities grade into more mesic, mixed-conifer types at their upper limits, and into grassland, big sagebrush steppe, or western juniper woodland at their lower limits [53,121]. Although “true savanna” may be rare, stands with widely scattered trees occur at lower treeline on rocky, exposed sites that are too droughty to support a dense tree canopy even in the absence of fire. In the Oregon pumice zone, extensive ponderosa pine woodlands occur in a mosaic with other forest types, especially lodgepole pine [121]. Lodgepole pine forests are covered in other FEIS Fire Regime publications.

#### Jeffrey Pine–Ponderosa Pine Woodland (10310)

These communities occur on relatively xeric sites in the montane zone on the eastern slopes of the southern Cascade Range [51]. While the two dominant pines tend to segregate by soil fertility and temperature regimes, they may occur together in certain areas on the Modoc Plateau. In the Carson Range and Warner Mountains, Jeffrey pine may be replaced by Washoe pine [121]. White fir may be present on relatively moist and cool sites. Common shrubs in these forests reflect a strong Great Basin affinity, including big sagebrush and antelope bitterbrush. Other common shrubs include greenleaf manzanita, curlleaf mountain-mahogany, birchleaf mountain-mahogany, and snowbrush ceanothus [23,121].

### East Cascades Mixed Conifer

Montane mixed-conifer forests and woodlands in the eastern Cascade Range occur between the drier, lower elevation ponderosa and Jeffrey pine zones and higher elevation subalpine zone [51,53]. These mixed-conifer forests correspond to the Douglas-fir and grand fir/white fir series described by Franklin and Dyrness [53] in Washington and Oregon, and lower-elevation Douglas-fir and white fir mixed-conifer forests of the southern Cascade Range in northern California [51]. Douglas-fir and grand fir series may be locally absent in the East Cascades; when they both occur, the grand fir series is found on relatively cooler, moister sites. The white fir series occurs from around Bend, Oregon, south to Crater Lake National Park. Grand fir and white fir hybridize extensively in central and southern Oregon [51,53] and are not always distinguished by species in the literature from that area.

Forests and woodlands in the mixed-conifer zone have more tree species than those in the ponderosa pine zone, although ponderosa pine and/or Jeffrey pine are common components and major seral dominants in mixed-conifer communities [51,53,140].

#### East Cascades Mesic Mixed-Conifer Forest and Woodland (10180)

These very productive communities occur on the upper eastern slopes of the Cascade Range in Washington, south to Mt Hood in Oregon, at 610 to 1,220 m elevation. Annual precipitation ranges from about 1,000 to 2,000 mm. Most are dominated by a mix of Douglas-fir with grand fir and/or western hemlock. Western redcedar, lodgepole pine, western white pine, and/or western larch may codominate. Sites with frequent fire were historically dominated by Douglas-fir and ponderosa pine. Common shrubs include Cascade barberry, Oregon boxwood, vine maple, thimbleberry, and thinleaf huckleberry. Herbaceous layer composition reflects local climate and degree of canopy closure and includes species such as vanilla-leaf, Columbian windflower, and white insideout flower [121].

#### Northern Rocky Mountain Dry-Mesic Mixed-Conifer Forest (10450)

Despite what the ecological system name implies, these forests also occur along the eastern slope of the Cascade Range in Washington and Oregon. Most of these forests are dominated by a mix of Douglas-fir and ponderosa pine, which historically maintained dominance due to frequent, low-severity fires. Associated and sometimes codominant species include lodgepole pine, western white pine, and western larch. These forests have understories dominated by graminoids such as bluebunch wheatgrass, pinegrass, Geyer’s sedge, and Ross’ sedge. Common shrubs include common juniper, mallow ninebark, common snowberry, white spirea, and thinleaf huckleberry [121].

#### California Mesic Mixed-Conifer Forest and Woodland (10280)

These communities occur throughout California, and are abundant on the eastern slopes of the southern Cascade Range between 1,700 and 2,100 m. They are composed of predominately white fir, with incense-cedar, Jeffrey pine, ponderosa pine, and Douglas-fir as codominants [51]. In the southern Cascade Range, this type is similar to mixed-conifer types in southern Oregon, and common shrub associates include ceanothus, manzanita, currant, and Pacific dogwood [93].

#### California Dry-Mesic Mixed-Conifer Forest and Woodland (10270)

These communities occur in small areas of the southeastern Cascade Range, and we did not find any fire histories in areas matching this description. They are composed predominately of Douglas-fir, ponderosa pine, and incense-cedar. Jeffrey pine, knobcone pine, and/or sugar pine may codominate. Common subcanopy trees include canyon live oak, California black oak, Pacific madrone, and tanoak. Common shrubs include oceanspray, hollyleaved barberry, and creeping snowberry. Understories are dominated by California fescue, blue wildrye, and California oatgrass [121].

## Historical Fuels and Fire Regimes

## Historical Stand Structure and Fuel Characteristics

Stand structure and species composition in historical ponderosa pine and montane mixed-conifer stands of the East Cascades varied substantially within and among sites due to variation in site characteristics, climate, topography, disturbance history, and successional stage [74,111]. Patterns on the landscape were driven largely by patterns of fire frequency and severity [61,134,166], and most historical stand reconstructions of these communities support the depiction of successionally diverse landscapes [74] with a large proportion (49%–76%) of low-density, open-canopy patches with sparse and patchy shrub layers and a grass-dominated understory [59,60]. Large, old fire- and drought-tolerant trees (mostly ponderosa pine) dominated both early- and late-successional patches [7,62,71,74,140]. Interspersed in this matrix were small, scattered clumps of young conifers with denser canopies, a more developed shrub layer, and a larger component of fire-sensitive species, as well as openings without trees [61,74,94,111,140]. These stand structures are compatible with an historical fire regime dominated by frequent, low- and mixed-severity fires, where high-severity fire effects were mostly limited to relatively small (<100-200 ha) patches [7,8,62,112,121,174], although Hessburg et al. (2005, 2007) provide evidence of stand structures suggesting some larger patches of high-severity fire during the early 20th century in the East Cascades of Washington that were likely driven by extreme fire weather [71,73].

Forests where mixed-severity fires historically dominated over space and time occupy a wide range of environments in the East Cascades [166]. Mixed-severity fires resulted in a patch-size distribution with many small and fewer large patches, contributing to a higher diversity of plant species and stand structures. Large areas of open-canopy forests and woodlands were most common on south-facing aspects, ridgetops, and lower montane elevations and were primarily maintained by frequent fires. Mid-montane elevations were a complex mixture of these open-canopy forests and denser, sometimes closed-canopy stands on north-facing aspects and valley bottoms, where fires varied from frequent to moderately infrequent and had a larger proportion of high-severity patches [74]. Structural patterns and fuel characteristics resulting from these fire regimes reinforced further occurrence of low- and mixed-severity fires, and spatially isolated fuel conditions that supported high-severity fires [71,111]. For example, areas adjacent to fuel-limited pumice basins where fire spread was inhibited, were more likely to have longer fire-free periods, and therefore, higher tree density and fuel biomass [111].

Historical ponderosa pine communities included a range of structural and fuel conditions: 1) widely scattered trees on relatively hot, dry sites where climate and soils limited tree growth, and sparse herbaceous fuels in shrub-steppe understories limited fire spread; 2) savannas and woodlands, where summer drought and frequent, low- and mixed-severity surface fires fueled by herbaceous understories maintained low and patchy cover of fire-tolerant shrubs and favored large, widely spaced trees with high crown bases and scattered, even-aged clumps of smaller trees; and 3) a small component of denser stands with abundant understory and ladder fuels, typically on relatively cool, moist sites such as northerly aspects, high elevations, and valley bottoms [71,121].

The shrub component varied in response to climate and fire, such that sites with low productivity or frequent fire lacked nonsprouting shrubs, and more productive sites or those with more variable fire characteristics maintained a well-developed or patchy and more diverse shrub layer [71,121]. For example, antelope bitterbrush is most common on sites with frequent fire [30]. Cover of herbaceous species was generally inversely related to tree density and cover, and—except on the driest sites—open stands typically had substantial grass and forb fuels that recovered rapidly after low-severity, surface fires [7,8,71,112].

Presettlement stand structures and fuel characteristics in mixed-conifer forests were likely similar to those of ponderosa pine communities in the East Cascades, although with greater variability. Mixed-conifer forests likely had a larger proportion of patches with intermediate-aged (50 to 150 years old), pole- to medium-sized, fire-tolerant trees (e.g., [73]) and more patches with substantial shrub cover and tree regeneration [59]. Mixed-conifer forests generally have greater available moisture and are therefore more productive than adjacent ponderosa pine communities [7,8,71,112,121], resulting in areas with greater fuel biomass and continuity, which are more likely to support mixed- and high-severity fire.

Stand reconstructions based on historical timber inventories depict mostly open-canopy landscapes dominated by large ponderosa pines (e.g., [38,59,60]). For example, historical inventories (completed between 1914 and 1925) of trees >15 cm diameter at 1.37 m ‘breast’ height (DBH) from two areas in Oregon, suggest the presence of open-canopy forests dominated by large (>53 cm DBH) ponderosa pines, with localized patches of higher tree density on moist mixed conifer sites dominated by white fir (on the Klamath Indian Reservation) [59], grand fir, or Douglas-fir (on the Warm Springs Indian Reservation) [60]. Historically, only 5% of the area exceeded 30% canopy cover, and canopy cover exceeding 50% was “almost nonexistent”, even in areas dominated by firs [59,60,61]. Large ponderosa pine trees made up most (81% ±20%) of the large tree basal area, which comprised 83% (±16%) of total tree basal area (BA) across a relatively wide moisture gradient, from dry ponderosa pine to moist mixed-conifer forest. Variability in composition and structure was greatest on moist mixed-conifer sites [59]. As moisture availability increased, canopy cover, tree density, basal area, and the relative proportion of Douglas-fir, white fir, and grand fir increased [59,60]. Areas with higher-than-average tree density (i.e., >120 trees/ha, or BA >25 m2/ha), although rare, were widely distributed across the study areas, and typically on relatively moist sites (e.g., north aspects). Large treeless areas were also rare, and mostly concentrated at higher elevations [60].

Hagman et al. (2017, 2019) [61,62] report that their reconstructions are consistent with multiple other sources, including three earlier surveys of the area (1866–1930), and with several studies that infer low historical availability of dense forests suitable for northern spotted owl habitat in the East Cascades. They also provide compelling evidence that Baker (2012, 2015) [19,20] overestimated historical stand density in these forests. Baker used General Land Office (GLO) survey data that relied on a much smaller sample of trees than the timber inventories used by Hagman et al. [59,60,61] (8 versus >3,000 trees/section). Findings from a separate study of mixed-conifer forests in the East Cascades of Washington that used GLO data to reconstruct historical densities [187] produced results comparable to other historical reconstructions (e.g., [59,60,63,112]). Their results indicated relatively low tree density and basal area, and large (>100 cm DBH) average tree sizes dominated by ponderosa pine. Nonetheless, reconstructions of historical vegetation from GLO survey data are subject to several potential sources of bias [187], and the methods and conclusions of Baker (2012, 2015) [19,20] have been challenged by others (e.g., [57,95,111]).

## Historical Fire Season and Ignition Sources

### Summary

Fire history studies in the East Cascades suggest that wildland fires could ignite as early as May and as late as October [30] but were most frequent in late summer and early fall (table 2). Ignition source may have influenced fire season. The fire history studies included in our synthesis did not address historical ignition sources except by citing earlier studies (e.g., [118,173]) and literature reviews (e.g., [7,8]), which suggest that most fires were ignited by lightning in the dry summer months. While American Indians also ignited fires in the areas covered by this synthesis, it is unclear how cultural burning affected ponderosa pine and mixed-conifer forests at a landscape level. Agee (1993) suggested that the frequency and timing of lightning storms were sufficient to explain much of the historical fire activity in the Pacific Northwest [7]. However, a case study at Fish Lake in the Sinlahekin Valley, Washington, suggests use of fire by American Indians had a strong influence on fire frequency in that area [175].

### Fire Season

Historically, most fires occurred in late summer and fall in both ponderosa pine and mixed-conifer forests. Across all forest types, fire was predominantly recorded in latewood (late growing season) or at the ring boundary (dormant season) (table 2), although there was some variability within studies. For example, most historical fires (1700-1849) recorded on sites on the Lassen National forest occurred in the late-growing and dormant seasons, although early growing-season fires were more common between 1680 and 1720, during the 1790s, and during an anomalously cool and wet period in the early 1800s. These authors speculate that early growing-season fires may have been ignited by American Indians in some years, but in years when fires were widespread, they were likely due to short-term climate variability (e.g., above-average precipitation in the years prior that increased fine fuel biomass, and/or drought during widespread fire years) [123].

Early growing-season fires (earlywood scars) were expected to be more frequent in ponderosa and Jeffrey pine forests than in mixed-conifer forests because fuels on lower-elevation sites desiccate earlier in the growing season and enable them to carry fire earlier in the year. This pattern was observed on sites in the southern Cascade Range [168], where the proportion of latewood and dormant season scars increased with elevation and available moisture [28]. However, the proportion of earlywood scars was similar among sites on both forest types (table 2). Only one study found that most fires occurred during the growing season: In a ponderosa pine forest fragmented by lava flows in the East Cascades of central Oregon, 75% of scars made between 1458 and 2001 occurred in earlywood. The dominance of early growing-season fires in this landscape may be related to steep south to west aspects with early snowmelt and drying of fuels, and topographic conditions conducive to convective storms. In this study a notable but unexplained shift toward dormant season fires occurred from 1880 to 1950 [16].

### Lightning-caused Ignitions

Lightning was historically the primary ignition source in these communities, and the frequency of thunderstorms and associated lightning varied regionally, interannually, and seasonally [3]. Information on historical thunderstorm and lightning strike frequency was not available for the East Cascades area specifically, and the fire history studies used in our analysis cited other sources (e.g., [7,8,118,173]) when discussing the frequency of lightning and ignitions in their area. Information on contemporary lightning frequency and distribution may reflect historical patterns.

The Pacific Northwest has less thunderstorm activity than some warmer parts of North America; however, much of it is concentrated in the warmest, driest months of the year (July and August) [7,118,168,173,187] and in locations east of the Cascade crest [3,8,118]—including parts of the East Cascades in northern Washington and central and southern Oregon [118].

While it seems that locations with a high frequency of lightning strikes would have a high frequency of fires [8], the percentage of lightning strikes that result in ignition (lightning ignition efficiency) is influenced by several factors, including fuel moisture, type, and abundance; vapor pressure deficit; and the amount of precipitation that accompanies lighting [3]. Thus, the number of ignitions and total area burned varies among seasons, elevations, aspects, fuel types, and storm characteristics [3,173].

The dry summer months have the highest frequency of both human-caused and lightning-caused ignitions in the East Cascades, and August has the highest potential for lightning ignitions [8]. In the Southern Cascade bioregion, 92% of lightning strikes between 1985 and 2000 occurred from May through September, with 59% in July and August [173]. Most lightning-caused fires occurred in July and August in the Cascade ecoprovince between 1992 and 2013 [3]. At Lava Beds National Monument almost half (45%) of the lightning fires occurred in July, and all others occurred in May (16%), June (9%), August (14%), and September (16%) [106]. Lassen National Forest fire records show that, over a 31-year period at the end of the 20th century, 47% of lightning ignitions occurred in August, 19% occurred in July and only 11% occurred in June [123].

Throughout the Pacific Northwest, midelevation areas seem to have the most fire starts, likely due to the combination of receptive (i.e., relatively dry) fuels and more frequent lighting. Within elevation zones, the upper one-third of slopes had the most ignitions, and southern aspects had more fire starts than northern aspects [7]. In the Southern Cascades bioregion, lightning strikes were most frequent between ~1,200 and 1,800 m, and the number of strikes dropped markedly above ~2,400 m and below ~600 m [173].

### Human-caused Ignitions

It is not possible to quantify the frequency or effects of historical or prehistorical anthropogenic ignitions, and the fire history studies included in our analysis did not address this topic. However, several authors have compiled information from other sources, such as historical accounts from tribal elders and early travelers to the area. From these accounts it is clear that humans have a long history of using fire to modify landscapes and tend the land in the Pacific Northwest, and that anthropogenic ignitions were widespread in the East Cascades before Euro-American settlement [7,8,31,100,144,155,156,162,165,175,183]. For example, a case study from Fish Lake, Washington, suggests a strong influence of American Indians on fire activity over the past ~1,200 years in that area [175]. Periods of high fire activity were coincident with periods of high population density. Similarly, the practical elimination of fire from the area occurred during the same time period as the decimation of American Indian populations by disease (by about 80%-90%) after first contact (around 1811), and the relocation of the few remaining survivors (~350) to the Colville Reservation by 1883 [175].

A long-standing history of burning by American Indians was certainly at least partly responsible for the open stand structure in ponderosa pine and mixed-conifer communities in particular locations, although the influence of human-set fires on the character of these ecosystems at a regional level is unclear [156,183]. American Indian populations in the forests of the East Cascades probably consisted mostly of small, seasonal hunting and gathering groups [183] who used fire to facilitate travel and hunting, to clear campsites, to improve grazing for horses, to communicate with other groups, and to promote the production and accessibility of desirable plants [7,31,183]. Although cultural patterns of fire use are regionally specific [31], in most cases fire was used to facilitate gathering food, such as driving or attracting game or creating environments suitable for edible and medicinal plants [7,30,31,106,141]. Many grasslands were anthropogenic in origin because they were burned regularly for these reasons [126], and it is likely that many fires spread from grasslands into adjacent woodlands and forests [31,106], whether intentional or not. Fires were also intentionally set in mixed-conifer forests to promote growth and productivity of fruit bearing plants such as thinleaf huckleberry [162].

According to Boyd (1999), American Indians burned seasonally, roughly following an elevational sequence that corresponded to plant availability, beginning with low-lying areas in May and early June and higher areas in late August through September [31].

### Fire Frequency by Forest Zone

#### East Cascades Ponderosa Pine

MFI values varied little among sites, despite differences in methodologies and time periods considered among studies. Presettlement MFIs (end dates ranging from 1849–1900) reported for 22 sites in the East Cascades ponderosa pine zone ranged from 5 to 24 years with a mean and median of 12 years (table 3). Historical MFIs (end dates ranging from 1849–1960) reported for 37 sites ranged from 5 to 25 years with a mean and median of 13 years (figs. 3 and 4).

The overall level of inference that can be derived from this data set is constrained by small sample sizes and limited information on study site characteristics and methodologies used, some of which influence the applicable scale of the metrics reported. Only 7 of the 25 mean values were composite MFIs [30,32,87,105,186], and most of these 7 came from studies that lacked information on site characteristics and sampling methodologies (e.g., [87,105,186]). In addition, nearly half (12 of 25) of the mean estimates were point MFIs derived from samples ranging from about 8 to 123 trees [105,154,157], while another 6 estimates were point MFIs calculated from single trees [88,178,179]. These point MFIs of 5 to 25 years would likely be even shorter if calculated for larger areas. Data for Jeffrey pine forests came from only two studies: 1) nine forest-meadow ecotone sites within a 70,000-ha area of Lassen National Forest [123] and 2) a 2,630-ha area on Prospect Peak in Lassen Volcanic National Park [168]. Several studies reported a composite MFI for the study area as a whole, in addition to site-level or plot-level means, indicating consistently shorter intervals for larger areas (e.g., [27,89,123,148,158,168]).

#### East Cascades Mixed-Conifer

Historical MFIs reported for 54 sites in the East Cascades montane mixed-conifer zone ranged from 4 to 100 years with a mean of 19 and median of 14 years (table 3). Mean and median MFI were similar to those in the ponderosa pine zone; however, variability in MFI was greater in the mixed-conifer zone due to a number of sites with considerably longer than average MFIs, including five statistical outliers that ranged from 46 to 100 years (fig. 3). Variability in estimates of historical fire intervals was expected to be greater for mixed-conifer than ponderosa pine communities due to greater variability in species composition, productivity, stand structure, elevational range, and moisture regimes among the mixed-conifer study sites. Variability within the mixed-conifer forest zone covered in this synthesis may also be due, in part, to numerical differences in sample sizes among the community types comprising the mixed-conifer forest zone.

Variability in MFI values among the 54 mixed-conifer sites (table A2) and among the three mixed-conifer communities (table 3) may be attributable, in part, to small sample sizes on some mixed-conifer sites and types. The most robust data set came from 27 sites classified as Rocky Mountain mixed-conifer; these data were predominantly composite MFIs derived from relatively large sample sizes ranging from about 10 to nearly 700 trees (about 90 trees on average), and mean MFI was shortest among mixed-conifer types (i.e., most similar to ponderosa pine types) [16,30,47,49,89,128,148,187]. In contrast, only 6 sites were classified as East Cascades mixed-conifer, and these had the longest mean and maximum MFIs [186].

### Fire Frequency by Site Characteristics

Patterns and variability in historical fire intervals were influenced by abiotic and biotic patterns and variability on the landscape that led to differences in moisture relationships, fuel productivity, exposure to fire spread, and frequency of ignitions. However, differences in methodologies used by the studies included in our analysis might obscure these patterns. Additionally, these studies did not typically report sufficient information about site characteristics to allow quantitative examination of these possible effects. The following is a review of the studies that analyzed the effects of site characteristics on fire frequency within their study areas.

Fires are generally less frequent at high elevations than low elevations, due to more precipitation and longer periods with intact snowpack that contribute to shorter fire seasons, higher fuel moisture, and a lower likelihood that fires will ignite and spread. This relationship may vary depending on location, topography, and the elevational range under consideration. Precise elevation information was lacking for many of the fire history study sites in the East Cascades, precluding an analysis of fire frequency by elevation. Several authors noted a general decrease in fire frequency as elevation increased in all or part of their study area (e.g., [47,108,148]). In the East Cascades of Washington, fire intervals were significantly longer in the highest elevation class (1,680–1,830 m) [148], and in the East Cascades of central Oregon, fire intervals were shortest in relatively mesic areas; both the higher-elevation, wetter sites, and the lower-elevation, drier sites burned less frequently. Drier sites were likely fuel-limited, except during or after relatively wet years [30]. In the southern Cascade Range, longer median fire intervals were correlated with higher elevations and greater relative soil moistures across all forest types in the Prospect Peak study area [168] and in the Thousand Lakes Wilderness [28]. In contrast, across 10,393 ha of ponderosa pine and mixed-conifer forest on the Deschutes National Forest, the shortest mean fire intervals from 1650 to 1871 were at lower elevations where impediments to fire spread were few and large fires were common, but also at upper elevations, which were on cinder cones isolated by pumice basins. Although lower fire frequency was expected on cinder cones because they are isolated from fire spread, the steep, prominent cinder cones have a combination of topography and fuels that increases the likelihood of lightning ignitions [111].

Site-level aspect and topographic features can affect fire frequency, both through their effects on fuel moisture, site productivity [28,47,49,52,148,168,186], and fire spread. In ponderosa pine and mixed-confer forests in eastern Washington, there is a tendency toward shorter fire intervals in areas of gentle topography and longer fire intervals in areas dissected by topographic barriers that limit fire spread [107,148]. On Prospect Peak, median fire intervals were shortest on eastern (6 years), longer on southern (13 years), and longest (25 years) on western aspects [168]. In contrast, Everett et al. (2000) found no significant differences in fire frequency between north and south aspects along two creeks on the Wenatchee National Forest, Washington [47], and Merschel et al. (2018) found no correlation between fire regime metrics (median fire interval, coefficient of variation in fire interval, number of small fires (<400 ha), number of large fires) and aspect or slope across 10,393 ha on the Deschutes National Forest, Oregon [111].

### Fire Frequency by Time Period

Plant community composition and fire activity on many East Cascades ponderosa pine and mixed-conifer sites varied considerably over the past ~15,000 years (table A3), with some notable patterns evident (see Historical Fire-Climate Relationships). Analyses of pollen and charcoal concentrations and characteristics in lake sediments throughout the Pacific Northwest region suggest that fire activity was low during the late Glacial period and started to rise at the beginning of the early Holocene (about 11,000–7,800 cal yr BP) (e.g., [50,104,184]). High fire activity in the region around 8,000 to 7,000 cal yr BP was linked to climatic and vegetation changes [177]. For example, peak fire frequency at Horsetail fen in central Washington between about 8,000 and 7,000 cal yr BP was attributed to warmer conditions and more biomass available for burning as vegetation shifted from a pine parkland/sagebrush steppe to a mixed-conifer forest [50].

Many sites in the Pacific Northwest record an increase in fire activity during the Medieval Climate Anomaly (MCA,1,100–700 cal yr BP), when climatic conditions were generally warm and dry, and a decrease in fire activity during the cooler, moister Little Ice Age (LIA, 500–100 cal yr BP) (e.g., [50,103,177]). A systematic review and analysis of thirty-four macroscopic charcoal records from a variety of biophysical settings in the Pacific Northwest showed that biomass burning gradually increased during the past 5,000 to 6,000 years (i.e., during the mid- to late Holocene) at both inland and coastal sites [177]. The increase in biomass burning during the Late Holocene was inconsistent with long-term trends in temperature and precipitation toward cooler and moister conditions in the region and might be better explained by greater climate variability (e.g., increased frequency of El Niño/Southern Oscillation (ENSO) events), changes in vegetation and fuel characteristics (e.g., [67,176]), and/or increased human use of fire prior to Euro-American settlement [50,67,147,176,177].

Abrupt declines in fire activity during the late 19th and early 20th centuries were evident in charcoal records from several lakes in the Pacific Northwest [177] and the East Cascades [67,102,175,176]. These declines coincided with decimation and relocation of American Indian populations, which resulted in fewer human-caused ignitions [90,175,177]. Other effects of Euro-American settlement on fire activity include widespread livestock grazing, which reduced fine fuel biomass and fuel connectivity, diminishing opportunities for fire ignition and spread (see Contemporary Fuels and Fire Regimes).

Several fire history studies compared MFIs during the presettlement period to MFIs in the settlement and fire exclusion periods [16,28,47,52,148,158,168]. Although sample sizes were generally too small to accurately detect differences among time periods and the specific dates used to define each period varied among studies, fire intervals tended to be shortest during the settlement period and longest during the fire exclusion period (table 4) [28,47,148,168].

### Frequency of Large Fires and Widespread Fire Years

Although many large fires were temporally independent [47], the largest fires often occurred during years or decades when fires were widespread throughout the region and contributed to larger-than-average area burned by wildfires in those years (see Historical Fire-Climate Relationships). Several fire history studies identified years in which large or widespread fires were evident—using a wide variety of detection criteria—and calculated their frequency (table A4, fig. 5). Across 15,997 ha on the Wenatchee National Forest, for example, large fires were episodic and were followed by a series of progressively smaller fires between 1750 and 1900. Annual area burned peaked during large fire years and then declined exponentially, reaching lows of around 300 to 1,000 ha per year. Area burned during large fire years ranged from around 3,200 ha to more than 12,000 ha across a 12,757-ha portion of the study area and from around 600 ha to 3,200 ha across a 3,240-ha portion [47]. From 1700 to 1918, in an 87,750-ha study area on the Pumice Plateau in southern Oregon—an area with few topographic barriers to fire spread—total area burned exceeded 20,000 ha every 9.5 years, on average. In 7 years during this time period, more than 40,469 ha burned (the current minimum size criterion of a 'megafire') [62]. In another (10,393-ha) area to the south, more than a third of the fires detected between 1650 and 1871 were at least 1,500 ha, and fires >4,000 ha were relatively frequent (MFI = 28 years) [111].

## Historical Fire Severity

Available evidence suggests that presettlement fire regimes in dry forests of the East Cascades were characterized by the dominance of frequent low-severity fires, with less frequent mixed-severity fires, and occasional, typically small, high-severity burn patches [28,73,74,77,111,134,170,175,187].

The past ubiquity of large, shade-intolerant, fire-scarred trees in uneven-aged stands indicates a presettlement fire regime dominated by frequent, often extensive, low-intensity surface fires with mostly low-severity effects to the overstory (e.g., [49,111,148,187]). However, few reconstructions sought evidence for high-severity fires (e.g., see [77]). Those that did noted occasional, mostly small patches (mostly <100–200 ha, up to about 1,000 ha) of single-aged cohorts that suggest past high-severity fire effects [47,62,73,77,111,187]. Taken as a whole, this evidence suggests that mixed-severity fires also occurred and even dominated the historical record in some locations and time periods (e.g., see [74,134]).

Two literature reviews provide detailed information about the ecology [134] and management [74] of mixed-severity fire regime forests in Washington, Oregon, and northern California. They define mixed-severity fire regime forests as those where mixed- or moderate-severity fires (where 20%-70% of the dominant tree basal area or canopy cover of a given patch of forest is killed by any single fire [7]) tend to dominate over space and time, but not to the complete exclusion of occasional low- (<20% overstory killed) or high-severity (>70% overstory killed) fires. Mixed-severity forest landscapes are characterized by complex mosaics with many small and few large patches that vary in successional and structural conditions (which, in turn, affect patterns of subsequent fires). Wildfire behavior in mixed-severity types is influenced by both local topography, vegetation type, and disturbance history, and by climate and weather. The characteristic pattern of patch sizes may be altered by extreme fire weather [134]. Evidence of large (>1,000 ha), high-severity historical fires seems to be absent from these zones (e.g., [47,62,77,111,187]), probably because relatively frequent surface fires maintained fuel characteristics that were not conducive to spreading crown fire (e.g., [71,148,164]). Information regarding historical frequency, pattern, and size of high-severity patches and mixed-severity fires is geographically limited; the most extensive datasets [62,111] come from the Pumice Plateau in south-central Oregon.

Studies using stand age data from the US Forest Service Forest Inventory and Analysis (FIA) records [127] and records from the General Land Office [19,20] have concluded that high-severity fire was more common and widespread in presettlement ponderosa pine and mixed-conifer forests than is suggested by other published reconstructions and evidence. However, the methods, assumptions, and conclusions of these studies have been challenged (e.g., [95,163]), and are not supported by the abundant evidence of low-severity fire (e.g., [62,111]). See Historical Stand Structure for additional details.

Only two of the fire history studies included in our analyses provided quantitative estimates of historical fire severity [28,170]. Both were located in mixed-conifer communities dominated by white fir in the southern Cascade Range in California, and each found evidence for a considerable proportion of moderate- (35%-46%) and high-severity (25%-63%) fire based on stand age-structures. However, severity patterns in white fir forests in the Thousand Lakes Wilderness were based on fires that occurred during a relatively short period (1864–1939), and were strongly influenced by large, high-severity fires in 1889 and 1918 that consumed tree-ring evidence of earlier fires. Because severity patterns in earlier fires could not be reconstructed, it is unknown whether these data reflect a persistent historical pattern for these forests [28]. Severity patterns in white fir forests in the Caribou Wilderness were based on a longer and earlier fire record (1735-1874) and showed a larger proportion of low-severity (29%) and smaller proportion of high-severity (25%) fires [170] than the Thousand Lakes study (2%-4% and 52%-63%, respectively) [28].

Evidence from studies not included in our analyses supports the idea that fires were predominantly low-severity in dry forests of the East Cascades (e.g., [62,73,111,175]), especially in ponderosa pine communities, but often varied with forest community type. For example, in eastern Washington and Oregon, evidence suggests a predominance of low-severity fires historically in dry ponderosa pine types and a predominance of mixed-severity fires (canopy mortality ranging from 20% to 70%) in mixed-conifer types [73]. Greater variability of presettlement fire-severity patterns in mixed-conifer communities is generally expected in landscapes with more topographic complexity and plant community variability, and at the wetter extremes of the mixed-conifer zone. In contrast, on simpler landscapes, such as the relatively gentle topography on the Pumice Plateau, Oregon, fire-severity patterns in mixed-conifer forests were mostly similar to those in ponderosa pine communities [62,111], except in areas adjacent to fuel-limited pumice basins, which were more likely to have relatively longer fire intervals, greater build-up of fuel, and higher subsequent fire severity [111].

High-severity patches were probably rare and mostly small (i.e., <100 ha) and rarely exceeded 1,000 ha [30,47,62,73,111,187]. For example, two studies on the Pumice Plateau in the Deschutes National Forest, Oregon, found that high-severity patches were rare and occurred as small patches (<100 ha) located within extensive low-severity burn patches [62,111]. Everett et al. (2000) noted that these patches appeared to be limited to individual stands or portions of stands where fire-free intervals were long enough that fuel accumulation was sufficient to carry fire into tree crowns [47]. Patches of stand-initiation structure—interpreted as prior high-severity fire—observed in early 20th century aerial photos across eastern Washington and parts of eastern Oregon were small (10-100 ha) to medium (1,000 ha) in size [73]. Bork (1985) observed a 400-ha patch of even-aged ponderosa pine and suggests that it may have established after high-severity fire [30]; however, others suggest that large patches of even-aged ponderosa pine may be associated with past insect outbreaks rather than fire (e.g., [7,62]). The pattern of many small and few large high-severity patches is fairly consistent within mixed-severity fire regime forests [134].

Although the pattern and causes of spatial variation in historical mixed-severity fire regimes are poorly understood [111], the size of high-severity patches and proportion of mixed-severity fires are generally thought to have been larger in areas with longer fire-free intervals [47,73,134,148], although see [111]. This relationship between fire severity and length of fire-free interval is also reflected in severity estimates based on BpS models, which show a larger proportion of high-severity fire and longer fire intervals in the most mesic BpS (East Cascades mesic mixed-conifer) (table A2). Stand development in such areas would have resulted in patches of relatively dense vegetation (i.e., more fuel accumulation and connectivity) with the potential to burn at higher intensity and result in greater tree mortality (i.e., higher severity), especially during dry periods [7]. Further evidence of this pattern includes reconstructions from 1650 to 1900 at three dry, mixed-conifer sites in eastern Oregon—one in the East Cascades, and two in the nearby Ochoco Mountains—that showed the most mesic forest types at each site had the greatest area burned at high severity [77]. In the East Cascades of Washington, high-severity fire was inferred from small, even-aged forest patches on mesic, north-facing slopes [47] and at a few locations in wet grand fir types [187].

## Historical Fire Pattern and Size

Except under extreme weather conditions [134], fire size is constrained by fuel connectivity and natural barriers to fire spread, such as changes in topography (e.g., when a side slope meets a riparian area or a ridgeline) and—often related—changes in plant community composition or successional stage (e.g., [47,111,148,170,187]). These barriers to fire spread likely defined edges of historical fires (e.g., [47,111,187]). However, topographic features may not always serve as effective fuelbreaks. For example, evidence from the East Cascades in Washington suggests that riparian areas may act as either conduits or barriers to fire spread, depending on plant community composition, valley type [148], and fire weather [134,145].

The size distribution of presettlement fires is difficult to determine, and therefore not well known. Evidence for both frequent [62,111] and infrequent large fires is presented in the published literature. Site-based fire history studies that used fire dates at sample point locations and topographic barriers to fire spread to estimate historical fire boundaries were available for a few East Cascades mixed conifer communities, but not for ponderosa pine communities. These studies provide evidence that suggests most historical fires were small (<400 ha), and larger fires were infrequent [28,30,148,168,170,187]. For example, between 1562 and 1995 in the Teanaway River valley, Washington, most fires (58%) were <1,500 ha. A small proportion of fires (16%) were >4,000 ha, and one was estimated at about 10,760 ha [187]. Mean and maximum fire size estimates from the southern Cascade Range in California are even smaller [28,168,170] (table 5).

This fire pattern would result in a landscape pattern with variable-sized but mostly small patches of vegetation that differed in successional stages and/or species composition, driven largely by the time since the last fire and the size and severity of that fire [47,73]. This pattern is observed in aerial photos from the early 20th century, which show a patch size distribution dominated by (88%) patches <100 ha. Many larger patches are also evident at this scale, and patches range from <4 ha to 3,373 ha and averaged 54 ha [73]. Larger patch sizes are thought to have resulted from rare or extreme events [134].

Exceptions to the pattern of mostly small fires and few large fires are documented in more recent, relatively large-scale studies in south-central Oregon, which show landscapes dominated by open-canopy (<30% cover) forests over broad areas in the East Cascades of Oregon [59,60,61,62], and provide evidence that fires in this area were frequently extensive (both large individual fire size and large area burned annually), especially in areas lacking barriers to fire spread [62,77,111]. For example, in the northern part of the Deschutes National Forest, Heyerdahl et al. (2019) found that most fires between 1650 and 1900 were “extensive” (>800 ha), low-severity fires that sometimes had small patches (<150 ha) of high-severity fire. The authors indicate that fires were likely larger than detected because they intersected sampling grid boundaries [77]. The largest fire detected on the Pumice Plateau (a landscape with few natural fire breaks) between 1871 and 1950 was 8,218 ha and included both ponderosa pine and mixed-conifer communities [111].

## Historical Fire-Climate Relationships

For ponderosa pine and dry mixed-conifer communities in the Inland Pacific Northwest, fire years in general, and widespread fire years in particular (see figure 6 for criteria examined), were most common during relatively warm, dry summers (El Niño/PDO+) at both site (e.g., [47,128,170,187]) and regional scales (e.g., [75,78,169]). In contrast, antecedent climatic conditions showed no distinct pattern at a regional scale (e.g., [75,78,169,187]) or at most sites (fig. 6). Exceptions were evident at two sites where drier than normal conditions occurred prior to widespread fire years [47,128]), and at some relatively dry, possibly fuel-limited sites where wetter than normal conditions occurred prior to widespread fire years (e.g., [123,135,136]).

Increased fire activity may be related to climatic factors operating over longer spatial and temporal scales than site-level, annual precipitation. For example, during the past 12,000 years in the Pacific Northwest, several periods of high climate variability (represented by the strength and frequency of ENSO events) generally coincided with evidence of increased fire activity, and several periods of reduced variability coincided with evidence of lower fire activity [177]. At Newberry National Monument, Oregon, widespread fires were related to reversals in the Pacific Decadal Oscillation (PDO) that were thought to be related to increased lightning ignitions as storm tracks migrate during switches of the PDO, which might also affect fire seasonality [16,136].

Spring climate in the Inland Pacific Northwest is influenced by large-scale climatic patterns such as ENSO (3–7-year phases) and the PDO (10–20-year phases), whose phases and interactions may influence the likelihood and extent of fires in a given year. Throughout the West, years with widespread burning are associated with the ENSO phase that corresponds with drier, warmer conditions—the El Niño phase in the Pacific Northwest [169]. However, fire studies from the East Cascades that included an analysis of widespread fire and the Southern Oscillation Index (SOI, a measure of ENSO variability) found that widespread fire years occurred during both La Niña and El Niño conditions [75,123,169,187]. The lack of strong relationships between fire and ENSO or PDO at individual sites may not reflect their role in synchronizing fire at regional scales [78] or the effects of their interactions. In the Pacific Northwest, a positive, warm-phase PDO is likely to amplify El Niño (warm/dry) effects, and a negative, cool-phase PDO is likely to amplify La Niña (cool/moist) effects [78,107,156,169]. For example, across 15 sites in the Inland Pacific Northwest (including 8 sites in the East Cascades), significantly more synchronous fire years (fire recorded at >6 of 15 sites) occurred from 1651 to 1900 when both ENSO and PDO were positive (warm phase) [78].

Analyses of fire-climate relationships using superposed epoch analysis (SEA) of Palmer Drought Severity Index (PDSI) and fire suggest that historical fire years—including but not limited to widespread fire years—were typically characterized by drier than average conditions (fig. 6). Negative PDSI values indicate relatively warm, dry conditions [75,78,169,187]. In the Teanaway River valley, Washington, fires occurred during wet, normal, and dry years, but large fires (>4000 ha) occurred when PDSI was significantly lower (drier) than average [187]. Across five sites in Washington, “regional” fires (those recorded on >10% of samples in all study sites) consistently occurred when PDSI values were negative [75], and across 15 sites in the Inland Pacific Northwest, regionally synchronous fire years (those recorded on >6 sites) consistently occurred when PDSI values were negative [78]. Across seven sites in the southern Cascade Range, widespread burning (≥10% and ≥25% samples scarred) between 1700 and 1800 was associated with negative PDSI during the fire year [169].

Patterns in climate conditions preceding fire years were less apparent at the regional scale (e.g., [75,78,169,187]), and showed no significant trends across sites (fig. 6), although patterns were evident at some sites (e.g., [47,123,128,136]). In most dry pine forests of the Pacific Northwest, increased growth of fine fuel in wet years is not necessary to precondition the landscape for widespread burning [75,76], and fires were more common during and after drought conditions [169]. Antecedent years with drier than normal conditions were evident at two sites (e.g., [47,128]). For example, tree-ring data indicated that relatively dry climatic conditions occurred during the 5 years prior to widespread fire years (1776, 1794, 1822, 1841, 1870, 1889) on the Okanogan-Wenatchee National forest [47]. However, on fuel-limited sites, consecutive or extreme years of above-average precipitation can enhance growth of woody plants and increase fine fuel accumulation and continuity, and thus increase chances for widespread fires during subsequent dry periods (e.g., [84,116,123,135,136,167]). For example, years preceding fire years tended to be wetter than average at Newberry National Monument [136] and in ponderosa pine forest-meadow ecotones on the Lassen National Forest [123]. Regionally, nonfire years were significantly cool-wet in the Inland Pacific Northwest [75,78]. This pattern was less evident on sites in the East Cascades [169], with some exceptions (e.g., [123]).

Over longer time scales and on many sites throughout the Pacific Northwest region (table A3), analyses of charcoal and pollen concentrations and characteristics in lake sediments suggest that changes in fire activity during the Holocene often corresponded with changes in climate and climatically controlled vegetation shifts, with the exception of the dramatic decrease in fire activity noted during and after Euro-American settlement (see Fire Frequency by Time Period). Increased fire activity generally coincided with relatively warm, dry periods and decreased fire activity coincided with relatively cool, moist periods on many sites [50,67,176,177,182]. For example, biomass burning at both high- and low-elevation, inland sites in the Pacific Northwest was greatest during the MCA and declined during the LIA [50,103,177], with some exceptions (e.g., [102]). However, variability among sites is high, and exceptions to this pattern are also evident on relatively dry (i.e., fuel-limited) sites—where fire activity increased following relatively wet periods [135]—and during the late Holocene—when increasing fire activity coincided with increasingly cool and moist climate and relatively high population densities [177]. At Green and Campbell lakes, located in a transitional zone where the Douglas-fir zone meets the ponderosa pine and sagebrush zones in north-central Washington, fire frequency during the late Holocene was apparently driven by fine fuel availability, which fluctuated in response to precipitation amounts and patterns [135].

## Contemporary Fuels and Fire Regimes

In some dry forest systems, settlement and management have resulted in continuous fuels prone to unrestricted fire spread. In other areas, fuel continuity has been fragmented and opportunities for fire spread restricted, especially in landscapes dissected by roads and housing, although opportunities for accidental human-caused fires have increased [73].

Fire regime disruption resulting from the many effects of Euro-American settlement—including decimation and displacement of American Indian populations and associated cessation of cultural burning (e.g., [90,175,177]), widespread livestock grazing and associated reduction in fine fuels [29,116,146], and widespread logging and associated fire suppression policies—has altered vegetation structure and composition throughout the region. Before widespread livestock grazing began in the mid-1800s, abundant and continuous cover of herbaceous fine fuels carried frequent surface fires in the East Cascades, often across large areas [29,32,62,111,178,179]. By the early 1900s, fires had become less frequent, due, in part, to overgrazing, and fire frequency continued to decrease with the advent of effective fire suppression [32,52,179].

Increased density of small trees is attributed to the coincidence of livestock grazing, fire exclusion, and the relatively mild, wet climate at the turn of the 20th century [112], followed by widespread logging throughout the Inland Northwest [70]. In grasslands, the absence of fire coinciding with the reduced abundance of herbs allowed tree seedlings to establish and spread [29,146]. In forests, extensive high-grade logging and clearcutting from the 1920s to 1980s removed large, fire-tolerant trees (Douglas-fir, ponderosa pine, and western larch) [70], and fire exclusion enabled shade-tolerant, fire-intolerant conifer species to proliferate [71] and led to development of even-aged, dense forests with fuels that can sustain high-severity fires [69] (see Contemporary Stand Structure and Fuel Characteristics).

Although fire frequency increased beginning in the late 20th Century (e.g., [96]), a substantial fire deficit persists in dry forests throughout the West. Contemporary fires continue to be much less frequent and extensive and have become proportionally more severe than presettlement fires [58,62]. For example, in ponderosa pine and mixed-conifer forests on the Pumice Plateau in southern Oregon, annual area burned between 1985 and 2015 was substantially lower than between 1700 and 1918, but a greater proportion of it burned with high-severity, stand-replacement effects [62].

Current stand conditions are vulnerable to the direct and indirect effects of seasonal and episodic increases in drought and fire, especially under a rapidly warming climate [58]. Between 1985 and 2010, wildfires burned about 10% of forests in the East Cascades. In both the warm ponderosa pine and cool mixed conifer zones, high-severity fire resulted in uncharacteristically large patches of sparse, early seral conditions, whereas deficits in low- and moderate-severity fire contributed to an increase in closed-canopy conditions despite these and other disturbances, such as logging and insect outbreaks [140].

## Contemporary Stand Structure and Fuel Characteristics

Stand structure, species composition, and fuel characteristics in contemporary ponderosa pine and montane mixed-conifer forests of the East Cascades differ from those in presettlement landscapes to the degree that Stephens et al. (2016) consider open-canopy forests dominated by large fire- and drought-tolerant trees “endangered” [163]. Dense forest conditions, once-rare, have become common, and open-canopy conditions with large trees, once common, are rare [140] (see Historical Fuels and Fire Regimes). Structure and composition in forests with longer presettlement fire intervals or mixed-severity fire regimes are more likely to be within their historical range of variation (HRV) [112].

Changes in forest structure and species composition after more than 100 years of fire exclusion and widespread logging—including species composition shifts, smaller average tree size, and multi-layered canopies—have resulted in increased fuel biomass, greater vertical and horizontal fuel continuity, with a greater proportion of intermediate-aged forest, and increased landscape homogeneity of species composition and stand structure [15,59,60,71,111,112]. These changes increase the likelihood of high-severity fire effects over large areas [62,74,110,113,140,179,187], especially in concert with climate change (e.g., [24,85,181]). Across the gradient from dry ponderosa pine to moist mixed-conifer zones, fire exclusion has led to advanced forest succession, resulting in fewer early-seral stands, an increased density of small-diameter (15-53 cm DBH), shade-tolerant trees, and fewer areas with open-canopy conditions. In addition, widespread logging has reduced the number of large (>50 cm DBH), old, fire-resistant trees [24,59,66,69,74,112,113,140,187]. Consequently, forest composition has shifted from a predominance of large, widespread, old ponderosa pines to small, highly dense, young, shade-tolerant tree species [59,83,112].

The formation of dense-canopies and understory cohorts of Douglas-fir, grand fir, and/or white fir increased fuel biomass, created fuel ladders [74], and decreased surface fuel flammability by changing forest composition from long-needled, relatively flammable pine litter and bunchgrasses to short-needled, less-flammable, compact fir litter with lower grass cover [111]. These canopy, surface, and ground fuel changes can increase the potential for high-intensity, high-severity crown fires [69,71,187] and decrease the likelihood and spread of low-intensity surface fires. Live fuel variables were the most important factors driving high-severity fire in the East Cascades ecoregion from 2002 to 2015 (average relative influence = 60.7%), followed by weather (averages for the day and year of the fire) (21.2%), topography (12.4%), and climate (averages from 1981-2010) (5.6%) [129]. In the early 21st century, 65% of forests in the mixed-conifer zone of the Oregon East Cascades have closed-canopies (≥40% canopy cover), and open-canopy forests dominated by large, fire- and drought-tolerant trees are rare [140]. Abundance of snags, logs, and canopy layers has likely increased in contemporary forests, whereas that of gaps, openings, and native shrubs and herbs has decreased [113].

## Contemporary Fire Season and Ignition Sources

Most contemporary fires and most area burned in the East Cascades and throughout the West still result from lightning ignitions during late summer and fall (July and August) [3,21,26,120]. In the East Cascades, most lightning strikes occur from May through September, and the highest potential for lightning ignitions is in July and August [3,8,106,120,123,173]. However, fire seasons have lengthened during the late 20th and early 21st centuries [181], due to combinations of increased frequency of human-caused ignitions, which can occur throughout the year [21,25,26]; and longer, more extreme periods of hot, dry weather caused by climate change (e.g., [5,41]), which results in earlier snowmelt and fire start dates in spring, later control dates in fall, and longer overall burn time for individual fires [181].

Lightning ignitions were largely responsible for increases in fire frequency and total area burned by large wildfires (>400 ha) in the western United States from 1970 to 2012. Less than 12% of the trend was due to changes in human-ignited wildfires [181]. Frequency of large, lightning-caused wildfires increased more in the Northwest Forested Mountains ecoregion (includes the Cascade Range, Sierra Nevada, and Rocky Mountains) than any other ecoregion in the West from 1992 to 2012, and lightning-caused wildfires accounted for 75% of the total area burned. Nonetheless, the proportion of human-caused wildfires (45%) was similar to that of lightning-caused wildfires (55%) [21]. On the Okanogan-Wenatchee National Forest and neighboring forest lands in the East Cascades of Washington, lightning-caused wildfires averaged 187.7 ha (range: 45,734 ha) and accounted for 92% of total area burned from 1997 to 2007, whereas human-caused wildfires averaged 16.7 ha (range: 6,646 ha) and accounted for 8% of the total area burned. Most area burned was by lightning fires in roadless and wilderness areas, and by fires >200 ha, regardless of ignition source [120]. Fires started by humans tend to be concentrated near roads and in areas surrounding large-population centers (i.e., the wildland-urban interface), and intensive-agricultural areas [26,54,120].

Fire seasons are lengthening due to both climate change (e.g., [5,41]) and human-caused ignitions, which can occur anytime receptive fuels are present (e.g., [21]). The average duration of large (> 400 ha) wildfires burned in the western United States increased from nearly 6 days from 1973 to 1982 to over 50 days from 2003 to 2012. The season for large wildfires increased from an average of 23 days from 1973 to 1982, to an average of 116 days from 2003 to 2012 in the Pacific Northwest (includes both sides of the Cascade Range, the Coast Ranges, and the Olympic Peninsula), and from 65 days (1973–1982) to 140 days (2003–2012) in the southern Cascade Range and Sierra Nevada [181]. From 1992 to 2012, human-caused fire season was 75 days, compared to a lightning-caused fire season of 36 days in the Northwest Forested Mountains ecoregion [21].

Most fire history studies examined in our analysis characterized fire season only for presettlement fires, although a few studies also included 20th-century fires in their fire record [16,168,187]. In the Teanaway River Valley, Washington, no change in the season of fire scar formation was found over the analysis period (1562-1995) [187]. In a forest fragmented by lava flows in central Oregon, the fire-scar record showed predominantly early growing-season fires, and dormant-season fires rarely occurred except during the period between 1880 and 1950. The cause for this shift is unclear, but the authors suggest it may correspond to a negative phase of the PDO from 1830 to 1920 or may be related to fuel patterns following recovery from widespread fires in 1823 and 1918 [16].

## Contemporary Fire Frequency

Contemporary fire frequency in East Cascades dry forest decreased dramatically in the early 20th century compared to the presettlement period. Fire history studies that compared fire frequency in different time periods generally found profound decreases in fire frequency and area burned after around 1900 [16,30,52,75,128,148], although specific patterns differed among sites (see Fire Frequency by Time Period).

Although the frequency of and total area burned by large wildfires (>400 ha) has increased since the 1970s (e.g., [181]), contemporary dry forests (in the West) show an overall fire deficit compared to presettlement frequency and area burned (e.g., [65,101,131]), particularly of low- and mixed-severity fire [62,65,101,139]. Throughout Washington and Oregon, dry forest ecosystems adapted to frequent, low-severity fire experienced an order of magnitude less area burned in the late 20th and early 21st centuries (1984-2015) than expected from presettlement reference conditions estimated from BpS models [65] and fire history studies [62,139,140].

## Contemporary Fire Severity

While total area burned by contemporary fires in East Cascades ponderosa pine and montane mixed-conifer forests remains much lower than that burned by presettlement fires, the relative proportion of area burned at high severity and size of high-severity patches in contemporary fires appears to be greater, based on comparisons of fire severity patterns in contemporary wildfires [35,62,65,79,101,111,115,139,140] to presettlement severity estimates from fire history studies [62,139,187] and BpS models [65,93,101] (table 6). Because contemporary forests experience less fire than presettlement forests [65,101,131] and the proportion burned at high-severity is greater, the average annual area burned at high severity in contemporary fires is similar to estimates from presettlement forests [101,139], although patch sizes of high severity effects are greater today. In contrast, annual area burned at low to moderate severity in these contemporary forests is orders of magnitude less than expected under presettlement fire regimes [65,101].

Between 1985 and 2015, only 5% of the Pumice Plateau ecoregion burned, and 23% of that area burned with high-severity, stand-replacement effects. Most stand-replacing fire (62%) occurred in 2002, the only year when area burned exceeded 20,000 ha. High-severity patches were thought to have rarely exceeded 100 ha historically [47,62,73,77,111,139,187]. In contrast, almost half of the area burned at high-severity between 1985 and 2010 in ponderosa pine and mixed-conifer communities throughout the Pacific Northwest occurred in patches >100 ha [139], and the two largest patches of stand-replacing fire on the Pumice Plateau, Oregon, between 1985 and 2015 were 1,799 ha in a ponderosa pine and dry mixed conifer stand, and 3,519 ha in a moist mixed-conifer stand [62].

Fire history studies that estimated patterns of presettlement fire severity in these ecosystems are limited, both in number and by inconsistencies in methodologies and size of area examined. Nonetheless, estimates for the mean proportion of high-severity fire in presettlement East Cascades ponderosa pine communities are consistently <20% [19,65,73]. Presettlement severity estimates based on models are similarly low (e.g., [65,93]), with two exceptions: 1) the driest ponderosa pine-dominated BpS, where fires were thought to have been infrequent, large, wind-driven events originating in adjacent shrub steppe or juniper woodland and resulting in mixed- and high-severity effects [93], and 2) some areas of montane mixed-conifer ecosystems, where estimates for mean proportion of high-severity fire in presettlement stands are much more variable, ranging from 14% to 63%. Contemporary fire-severity patterns are more likely to resemble presettlement patterns in these areas with more complex fire histories and topographies. For example, from 1984 to 2010, throughout the western United States, mixed-conifer forests were associated with higher severity fire, and topography had moderately strong effects on both fire occurrence and severity [98].

Temporal trends toward increased area, larger patches, and increased proportion of high-severity fire were not evaluated for East Cascades ponderosa pine and mixed-conifer ecosystems, specifically. However, temporal trends toward larger patches of high-severity fire in the Pacific Northwest were evident in forest zones with historically low- and mixed-severity regimes and were related to drought and annual fire extent [139]. Increases are also evident from these ecosystems in the southern Cascade Range (e.g., [114,115,161]).

Increased frequency and extent of high-severity fire in dry forests of the East Cascades is likely due to a combination of increased fuel biomass and connectivity, and more severe fire weather resulting from climate change (e.g., [190]). Increases in fuel biomass and connectivity after decades of fire exclusion make contemporary wildfires more likely to burn at high severity than historically [63]. Fuel characteristics in young, intensively managed plantations on private industrial lands are also prone to high-severity fire effects and a concern for fire spread [190]. At a regional scale, high-severity fire from 1984 to 2010 was related more to fuel biomass and connectivity than fire season aridity (e.g., [129,132]). In the driest forests, antecedent climate may influence fire occurrence and behavior by promoting growth of fine fuels (e.g., [35,42]). In contrast, topographic variables were relatively more important predictors of severe fire occurrence than either climate or weather variables from 1984 to 2006 in the Inland Northwest ecoregion (includes the East Cascades, Modoc Plateau, and the Blue/Ochoco/Wallowa mountains) [42].

Models developed to evaluate change in vegetation composition and fuel biomass across the western United States, including modeled points located in the East Cascades, indicate fire severity and frequency may increase in future decades [97,130]. Increased fire severity may occur due to higher fuel biomass and lengthening fire seasons, with fire danger becoming more extreme [85]. However, by the mid-21st century, more frequent fire may lead to lower fire severities for large portions of the western United States [97,130]. This is attributed to high water deficits that result in low plant productivity and consequently, less burnable biomass [130].

## Contemporary Fire Pattern and Size

Fire chronologies from dry forest sites in the East Cascades generally show less frequent and smaller fires during most of the 20th century, in comparison with presettlement fire patterns (e.g., [62,187]). In the latter part of the 20th century, area burned by wildfire increased dramatically across the western United States, with large wildfires accounting for most of the area burned [1,96,114,115,181].

In the East Cascades of Washington, large fires in roadless and wilderness areas accounted for most of the burned area between 1997 and 2007. Most small fires occurred in roaded areas and accounted for only a small proportion of the total burned area. From the standpoint of total area burned, the effect of forest roads on restricting fire size is likely greater than the impact of roads on increasing fire ignitions (e.g., [120]). Across the United States, most area burned by wildfires from 1992 to 2015 was in large lightning-caused fires (56%), followed by large human-caused (36.4%), small human-caused (6.6%), and small lightning-caused (0.9%) fires. Large fires of both human and lightning origin occurred coincident with hot, dry weather compared with small fires. Large human-caused wildfires tended to coincide with high wind speeds that contributed to rapid fire growth [1].

## Contemporary Fire-Climate Relationships

Wildfire area burned in the American West was controlled by climate during the 20th century (1916–2003) in ways similar to those suggested for presettlement fires [96]. Annual area burned in the West, as a whole, is related to temperature effects on snowmelt timing and fire-season length [180,181] and to aridity during the fire season [2,3,80,98,142]. Most mountainous ecoprovinces show strong year-of-fire relationships with low precipitation, low PDSI, and high temperature; and grass- and shrub-dominated ecoprovinces show positive relationships with antecedent precipitation or PDSI. Linkages between climate and area burned may vary among time periods (e.g., were stronger for 1977–2003 than for 1916–2003) and ecosystems [96].

Throughout most of the ponderosa pine and montane mixed-conifer zones in the East Cascades, fuels are usually dry enough during the summer to burn [35,79,111]. Nonetheless, anthropogenic climate change contributes substantially to fuel drying, erratic and extreme weather patterns, and associated increases in fire activity throughout forests in the West, and it will likely continue to do so where fuels are not limiting. During fire season, dry fuels contributed to an average of 9 additional days per year of high fire potential from 1979 to 2015 [5], and small increases in fire extent over time (1985–2010) were associated with drought in all vegetation zones of the Pacific Northwest [139].

Increases in the number of large wildfires (> 405 ha) and total annual area burned by large fires increased in the western United States and in most Level II ecoregions from 1984 to 2011, coinciding with trends toward increased drought severity [45]. Several studies examined the relationship between climate and the increased frequency of large and very large contemporary wildfires in the western United States (e.g., [5,45,80,96]), although data or observations specific to the East Cascades are lacking.

### Future Fire-Climate Relationships

Climate warming in the East Cascades has already led to drier and longer fire seasons, rain replacing snow in many precipitation events, declining snowpack, and an upward migration of the elevation of the freezing line [4,101]. Projections from climate models developed for the Northwestern United States, indicate minimum and maximum monthly temperatures in the Cascade Range will continue to increase [153], with the greatest increases in winter and spring [37]. Associated decreases in snowpack and increases in evapotranspiration are expected to increase the frequency and severity of both drought and insect outbreaks, (which may, in turn, elevate fire risks) [37,64]. Changes in available soil moisture can alter forest composition and structure, especially in conjunction with increased wildfire occurrence [153]. Projected changes in winter precipitation from snow to rain may dramatically increase drought stress and decrease fire-season fuel moisture in these forests [64].

Greenhouse gases, which contribute to climate warming, may increase cloud-to-ground lightning strikes [137]. Fire frequency could increase with predicted increases in lightning strikes across the East Cascades if climate conditions remain conducive to fire ignition and spread [3].

Models combining spatiotemporal ignition patterns in the East Cascades of Oregon under contemporary (2006−2015) conditions with global climate models of mid-century conditions (2031–2060) project a 14% decrease in lightning-caused ignitions, and a 31% and 22% increase in fire size for human and lightning-caused ignitions, respectively, by 2060. All but one climate model projects increased frequency of record-breaking events relative to the contemporary period, with the largest future fires being about twice the size of those of the contemporary period [25].

## Fire Management Considerations

Stand structure, species composition, and fire regimes in ponderosa pine and dry mixed-conifer forests in the East Cascades are outside the HRV on most sites, to the extent that some authors consider them endangered ecosystems [163]. Widespread fire deficits (see Contemporary Fire Frequency) have resulted in heavy fuel loads over large areas (see Contemporary Stand Structure and Fuel Characteristics) conducive to large burn patches with high-severity fire effects (see Contemporary Fire Severity), and a high potential for fire spread into the Wildland-Urban Interface (WUI) and adjacent communities [12].

Hazardous fuel reduction is needed to mitigate fire spread and severity in and adjacent to the WUI (e.g., [12]), to protect the few remaining patches of old and large-diameter dry forests [68], and to restore resilient dry forest ecosystems throughout the West. Managing for landscapes with small- and large-scale variability in forest structure, species composition, successional stages, and fuels is important for achieving patterns and patch size distributions that are more similar to historical patterns and therefore presumed to be more resilient to fire and other disturbances. Reducing fuels by thinning trees to achieve “average” conditions that may favor low-severity fires is insufficient for achieving heterogeneous and resilient landscapes [73,159]. Average conditions may mask significant variability and are an oversimplification of historical conditions. Restoring heterogeneity at multiple spatial scales is essential for restoration in these ecosystems (review by [60]). Charnley et al. (2017) explore how diversity in ownership and management might affect landscape heterogeneity and resilience to high-severity fire in ponderosa pine and dry mixed-conifer forests of south-central Oregon [36].

Wildfire and prescribed fire are important tools for reducing hazardous fuels and achieving landscape heterogeneity and resilience [73,100,125,130]. It is important to consider the effects of contemporary climate and projected climate changes on fire behavior and fire effects when managing with fire [73].

## Fire Deficit

Fire was infrequent in East Cascades dry forests during most of the 20th century and, although fire frequency and area burned increased in the late 20th and early 21st centuries [58,181], they are still much lower than in the presettlement era, reflecting an overall fire deficit (e.g., [65,101,131]). These trends are driven by large deficits of low- and moderate-severity fire in frequent-fire forests (see Contemporary Fire Severity). These deficits have contributed to widespread changes of forest structures and ecological functions [58,140].

Restoration in these forests requires management that facilitates much more low- and moderate-severity fire. Simply increasing wildfire extent while maintaining current trends in burn severity (i.e., high proportions of high-severity burn area) threatens ecosystem resilience and could drive undesirable ecosystem transformations [65].

## Fuels Management

A lot of work and attention has been given to the methods and effects of hazardous fuels reduction in dry forests of the western United States (e.g., [13,82,86,109,172]), the Pacific Northwest (e.g., [10,12,14,43,138]), and the East Cascades (e.g., [44,46,91,122,133]). Objectives of fuel treatments include lowering the risk of large, high-severity burn patches and threats to human infrastructure (e.g., [11]), protection of remaining old-growth, large-diameter stands [68], and restoration of multi-scale habitat heterogeneity [74].

Given that these forests are characterized by both low-severity and mixed-severity fire regimes, fuel and fire management objectives are likely to vary among sites within these forest types [74]. For example, forests with a history of mixed-severity fire regimes may require more complex management approaches [74,134].

Some authors have suggested that the risk of loss of dense multistoried forest to high-severity fire is relatively low, and that restoration activities on any significant scale are unjustified because large patches of high-severity fire are within the HRV for these forests (e.g., [19,20]). However, their inferences are based on a methodology that has been criticized (e.g., [57,95,111]) (see Historical Stand Structure and Fuel Characteristics), and available evidence strongly suggests that the dense, multistoried forests that dominate these landscapes in the early 21st century are over-represented on the contemporary landscape, and increase the risk for high-severity, stand-replacement fire (see Contemporary Stand Structure and Fuel Characteristics).

The goals of restoration fuel treatments are not necessarily to eliminate high-severity fire, but to have it resume a more characteristic role at appropriate spatial and temporal scales [74], which in these forests includes infrequent and relatively small (<100 ha) patches (see Historical Fire Severity). In contrast, large (<2,020 ha) and very large (>4,050 ha) high-severity fires, as well as megafires (>40,500 ha) are expected to increase in frequency with climate change [6,22].

### Role of Wildfire and Prescribed Fire

Wildfires and prescribed fires are important components of contemporary fire and fuel management in dry forests of the East Cascades (e.g., [13,24,43,44,68,81,86,138]). Contemporary wildfires are getting harder to suppress [34,62], and aggressive wildfire suppression contributes to the increased extent, intensity, and damage associated with those large wildfires that cannot be suppressed [34]. Instead, wildfire effects may help achieve management objectives on some sites. Management of lightning-caused wildfires for ecological benefit, also called “restoration wildfire” (among many other things)—where fires are managed to burn under specific conditions—has been recommended to help increase forest resilience by reducing fuels, improving habitat, and restoring seral stand structure [24,125,163], in part because the number of hectares needing hazardous fuels treatments cannot realistically be achieved with mechanical treatments and prescribed fires alone (e.g., see Haugo et al. 2015 [66]).

The effects of wildfire at stand scales (10 to 100 ha) are highly variable but generally include decreased tree density, basal area, and canopy cover. High-severity fire creates early-seral conditions, while low-severity fires may create or maintain open canopy conditions. This type of variation in fire effects at stand scales promotes heterogeneity at landscape scales (1,000 to 10,000 ha), where a mosaic of structural conditions and seral stages (i.e., early vs. old-growth) is desirable [140]. Contemporary wildfires burning under moderate weather conditions, can provide heterogeneity in vegetation cover and structure, which may mitigate fire size and burn severity in subsequent wildfires [72,164]. In the 2006 Tripod fire in the East Cascades of Washington, about 3% of the total burned area had burned 3 to 12 years prior. Burn severity was generally lower in these areas, probably due to lower fuel biomass and connectivity. Burn severity increased as time since fire increased, particularly in dry forests [164].

Analyses of 99 large wildfires (≥400 ha) in forest-dominated landscapes in Washington and Oregon that burned only once from 2004 to 2015—including 153,763 ha of mixed-conifer and 79,818 ha of ponderosa pine forests—found that fire refugia—areas within forest fire perimeters that remain unburned or burn at low severity—were generally most abundant where high- and very high-severity fires were least abundant (and vice versa). In mixed-conifer forests, refugia made up about 29% of the total extent, and high and very high severity patches made up about 27%. In ponderosa pine forests, refugia accounted for about 30% of the area and high and very high severity patches about 25%. Severity varied with prefire structural conditions; wherein generally higher severity fire occurred in areas with moderate to high tree cover dominated by small to medium sized trees [110].

Under some conditions, wildfires may be managed to increase the pace of forest restoration and improve forest resilience. Simulations of restoration wildfires (i.e., lightning-ignited fires started under mild weather conditions) on the Deschutes National Forest suggest that restoration wildfire can improve forest resilience and contribute to restoration efforts in fire-adapted forests. Simulations that include one restoration wildfire per year in addition to high-risk wildfires (i.e., fires that threaten human values) in the regular fire season resulted in a 23% increase in total area burned over the course of 50 years, but the same probability of fire-on-fire interactions that moderate fire spread and intensity of future fires. Trade-offs identified included smoke and possible undesirable impacts to wildlife habitat and fire-sensitive forest types [24]. In contrast, contemporary wildfires in ponderosa and mixed-conifer forests on the Pumice Plateau, do not appear to be restoring fire-resistant forest structure. For example, large-tree density is especially low in an area burned by high-severity fire in 1992 and subsequently salvage logged [62].

Prescribed fires and cultural burning are important tools for restoration of dry forests in the East Cascades. Tribes in the Pacific Northwest have long depended on fire to steward their environments, and they are increasingly asserting the importance of cultural burning to achieve goals for ecological and social restoration [100]. Prescribed fire is also used to manage fuels and reduce wildfire risk associated with fire exclusion in contemporary ponderosa pine forests throughout the western United States (e.g., [13,24,43,44,68,86,138,185]). Prescribed burning combined with thinning typically reduces subsequent fire severity across a variety of dry forest types in the Pacific Northwest, and implementation of the 2022 Wildfire Crisis Strategy in the Pacific Northwest specifies employing these treatments. However, challenges to implementation include limited windows of appropriate weather conditions, the necessity for long-term planning and repeat treatments, limited workforce capacity, and litigation delays [171]. In addition, changing climate and increasing human encroachment into the wildland–urban interface may limit the use of prescribed fire in the future [185].

## Climate Change Considerations

Climate and weather are important considerations when managing wildfire and prescribed fire effects (see Contemporary Fire-Climate Relationships). Longer, hotter, drier fire seasons are associated with greater area burned by high-severity wildfires in contemporary dry forests of the West (e.g., [55,181]). These conditions and relationships are projected to continue under future climate scenarios [33,56,97,152,185]. Syntheses about climate change, fire, and forest management in the Pacific Northwest are available (e.g., [56,185]). These summarize available science on projected changes and assess how forest management can aid in adapting to and reducing negative impacts of future fire regimes [185].

High rates of tree mortality (e.g., from high-severity fire and drought) and low rates of regeneration may lead to major changes in species dominance and vegetation type in some low-elevation ponderosa pine and mixed-conifer forests [6,39]. Increasingly hot, dry postfire weather may interfere with postfire tree seedling establishment and succession, especially on large, severely burned sites [6,39].

The degree of departure from historic stand structures, species composition, and fire regimes complicates climate change adaptation [65]. Even in the absence of fire, many contemporary ponderosa pine stands are dominated by grand fir and Douglas-fir, species that may not be able to tolerate future climatic conditions [64].

Anticipating widespread fire years and seasons can help with fire planning and management. Stavros et al. (2014) provide a means for predicting the probability of very large wildfires (>20,000 ha) occurring in a given week, and thereby the timing of substantial area burned within a given year [160]. Long-term fire planning using the PDO may be possible in the Pacific Northwest, potentially allowing decadal-scale management of fire regimes and vegetation dynamics [107]. Estimates of water storage and supply in forests could also assist in planning to prepare for a changing climate by using indicators of low soil moisture and high evaporative demand to locate areas with increased wildfire risk and increased vulnerability to insect attacks and disease [143].
