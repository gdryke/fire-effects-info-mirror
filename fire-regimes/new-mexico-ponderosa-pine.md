## Summary

This fire regime synthesis details characteristics of ponderosa pine ecosystems from fire history studies conducted in two ecoregions in New Mexico: the Southern Rockies ecoregion (hereafter, Southern Rocky Mountains) and the Arizona/New Mexico Mountains ecoregion (hereafter, New Mexico Mountains). Together, fire frequency, fire severity, fire extent, fire seasonality, and the fire-climate relationship provide a cohesive interpretation of the dominant historical fire regime (i.e., prior to Euro-American settlement) and how the contemporary fire regime differs. Fire frequency data were obtained from 108 sites using fire scars dating from 1513 to 1993. The overall mean fire interval (MFI) was 9.3 years (n = 108 sites) with very low variability among sites (SE = 0.58 years). Fire frequency and site elevation were weakly correlated (r = 0.18), and although the slightly higher-elevation Southern Rocky Mountain sites had a longer mean fire interval (mean elevation = 2,540 m, MFI = 11.2 years, n = 54 sites) than the New Mexico Mountains sites (mean elevation = 2,386 m, MFI = 7.3 years, n = 54 sites), the difference was not statistically significant.

The vast majority of evidence shows fires were frequent (94% of sites had MFI < 20 years), which strongly suggests that low-severity fires were the dominant type. Higher severity fires occurred less frequently - particularly during extended periods of drought or during severe drought. These droughts were also associated with widespread fire years.

Most historical fires seem to have occurred in spring and early summer, prior to the onset of monsoon rains. Most fire scars from 9 sites in the Southern Rocky Mountains and 21 sites in the New Mexico Mountains were in dormant and early-earlywood positions (min 51%, max 97%, mean 69%, n = 8 studies), which are attributed to fires occurring between April and June. Fire seasonality data spanned six centuries (from 1296 to 1903), suggesting a persistent relationship between pre-monsoonal dry conditions and early season lightning ignitions.

The fire-climate relationship was similar between the two ecoregions with all results indicating drier-than-average conditions during the fire year and wetter-than-average conditions dominating in the 4 antecedent years. The pattern of wetter-than-average conditions in the immediate years preceding a fire year is indicative of a fuel limited system, whereby biomass production that results from increased moisture availability is desiccated and burned in subsequent drier-than-average fire years.

The dominant fire regime characterized by frequent, low-severity fires has shifted to less frequent and higher severity crown and stand-replacing fires over recent decades. Both the area burned at high severity and the percentage of high-severity fires increased in New Mexico ponderosa pine forests from 1984 to 2015.

## Introduction

Fire regimes broadly describe the role of wildfire in shaping the structure and function of ecosystems. Together, multiple factors including fire frequency, severity, extent, and seasonality provide a comprehensive view of the role of fire, and these factors vary spatially and over time. Understanding how these factors vary among plant communities and with climatic conditions, elevation, and geographic position within plant communities further enhances our understanding of the influence of fire regimes on historical and contemporary ecological settings. This knowledge can be used to better understand how forest management and other human activities affect the complex interactions of fire regime characteristics and environmental factors over time.

The objective of this report is to synthesize published information on the characteristics of historical and contemporary fire regimes in ponderosa pine communities in two ecoregions in New Mexico. This information can be useful for forest management planning, fire management decision making (especially prescribed burning), and ecological research.

## Distribution

## Study System

The climate where ponderosa pine occurs in New Mexico is characterized by cool winters and warm summers, with mean annual temperatures ranging from 5 to 7 °C. Mean annual precipitation ranges from 520 to 660 mm in ponderosa pine forests, with most falling as snow in winter and as rain in summer [33]. The broader region is influenced by the North American Monsoon System, which typically causes a dramatic increase in precipitation from thunderstorms in the summer (mostly July and August). Mean precipitation in New Mexico for July through August (1950¬–2017) is 122.5 mm, but daily and interannual variability in precipitation is high during these summer months. For example, July through August precipitation in 2006 was 202.9 mm compared to only 58.4 mm in 2003 [28]. An inverse relationship between monsoon precipitation and precipitation in the preceding winter has been observed (data from 1963–1994), where monsoons with above-average precipitation follow winters with below-average precipitation, and vice versa [18]. Likewise, a strong negative linear relationship (r = -0.61) between summer precipitation in New Mexico and prior spring snowpack in the southern Rocky Mountains was identified over a similar time period (1961–1990) [15].

Ponderosa pine ecosystems in New Mexico experience a bimodal distribution of precipitation with monsoonal summer (June–August) rain, and cool-season (October–April) precipitation. Both monsoon and cool-season precipitation amounts were highly variable in the region over the past 5 centuries [10]. Drought severity increases when both seasons are congruently dry, or when there is an extended period of drought in any one season. Palaeoclimatological reconstruction reveals instability in the relationship between monsoon and cool-season precipitation over time, and it identifies periods when both seasons had below-average precipitation. For example, in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, there was a prolonged period of monsoonal drought where 19 out of 24 years had below-average monsoon precipitation overlapping a similar persistent and strong cool-season drought [10]. The “megadrought” from 1566 to 1587 had below-average precipitation in both seasons for 10 of the 22 years, and at least one of the seasons had below-average precipitation in 21 of the years. This period is among the most severe droughts of the last millennium [10] and has been implicated with broad-scale ecosystem changes throughout the Southwest [37].

Ponderosa pine is the dominant or codominant canopy tree species throughout much of montane New Mexico (fig. 1), and it may be identified as either southwestern ponderosa pine (P. p. var. brachyptera) or Rocky Mountain ponderosa pine (P. p. var. scopulorum). These varietal designations are not common in the literature, and hereafter I use “ponderosa pine” to refer to both, and direct readers to the FEIS Species Review of Southwestern and Rocky Mountain ponderosa pine for detailed information on natural history and fire ecology.

This fire regime synthesis addresses information derived from fire history studies in ponderosa pine ecosystems in the New Mexico portions of the Southern Rockies (hereafter, Southern Rocky Mountains) and Arizona/New Mexico Mountains (hereafter, New Mexico Mountains) Level III ecoregions [42], excluding sites in the southwestern part of the state (e.g., Gila National Forest) because that area aligns more closely with ponderosa pine ecosystems in Arizona. Fire history studies from these areas often include information from study sites in both states, rendering it difficult to separate information.

The Southern Rocky Mountains ecoregion reaches its southern extent in northern New Mexico, terminating near Santa Fe, and is comprised of two large mountainous areas that are separated by the Rio Grande Valley and the New Mexico Plateau ecoregion. The New Mexico Mountains ecoregion is represented by a series of discontinuous mountains extending from the northwest to the south-central part of the state. The climate in the Southern Rocky Mountains ecoregion is cooler and wetter (mean annual temperature from -4°C at high elevations to 11°C in lowlands, and mean annual precipitation of 588 mm) than the New Mexico Mountains ecoregion (mean annual temperature from 3°C at high elevations to 19°C in the lowest southern valleys, and mean annual precipitation of 477 mm).

Ponderosa pine sites ranged from 2,032 m to 2,985 m in elevation among the studies considered in this synthesis. In the Southern Rocky Mountains ecoregion, ponderosa pine is found in pure stands from about 2,100 m to 2,300 m, and often adjacent to and above pinyon-juniper woodlands, which occur from 1,900 m to 2,100 m. Ponderosa pine stands transition to more diverse mixed-conifer forests containing a significant component of ponderosa pine growing in association with Douglas-fir, white fir, aspen, limber pine, and southwestern white pine from 2,300 m to 2,900 m [3]. Long plateaus, deep canyons, and high mesas characterize much of the mountainous environment where ponderosa pine and dry mixed-conifer forests occur. Embedded within these extensive forests are large shrubfields (> 100 ha) dominated by Gambel oak with components of New Mexico locust, trumpet gooseberry, and perennial grasses. These shrubfields can be very old (> 500 years) and represent alternative stable states to the surrounding coniferous forest community [30].

## Historical Fuels and Fire Regimes

The fire regime parameters most frequently addressed by fire history studies in ponderosa pine ecosystems and evaluated here are: 1) Fire frequency—the number of fires per unit time in a given area; reported as the average number of years between successive fires over a given time period (i.e., mean fire interval, hereafter MFI). Fire frequency in ponderosa pine ecosystems is derived from dendrochronological techniques that identify fire scars on tree rings and record the corresponding year of fire. 2) Fire severity—generally indicates the degree of change caused by fire, such as the relative proportion of trees killed within a given area (e.g., the fire perimeter or patches within), and is typically classified and reported as high, moderate, low, or mixed. 3) Fire extent—the size of the area burned by an individual fire, the distribution of individual fire sizes, or the total area burned by all fires within a specified time period. 4) Seasonality—the time of year a given fire occurred. In dendrochronological studies, the position of a fire scar within annual growth rings approximates the time of year that fire occurred. 5) Fire-climate relationships—climate has a strong influence on fire regimes through its effects on fuel abundance, type, and moisture content. This relationship is examined using an analysis that integrates multiple lines of evidence to infer precipitation conditions during and prior to fire years.

## Methods

### Literature

I searched the databases of the Citation Retrieval System of the U.S. Forest Service Fire Effects Information System, Google Scholar, and Web of Science for published and unpublished studies using the following keywords: New Mexico, fire regime, fire, ponderosa pine. I then searched the literature cited of relevant studies to identify additional related papers that may have been missed in the database searches. The search process concluded on 24 July 2019, and in total 99 studies were identified for possible inclusion. Studies were reviewed and included if they met the following criteria: research occurred in ponderosa pine ecosystems in New Mexico, addressed at least one of the five fire regime parameters of interest (frequency, severity, extent, seasonality, or climate relationships), was empirically based (i.e., numerical results provided based on field observations), and was not entirely simulation, modeling, risk or hazard analyses, speculation, or opinion. Studies where research occurred in the southwestern part of New Mexico were excluded because ponderosa pine ecosystems in that region are more similar to those in Arizona. Of the 99 papers originally identified, 55 met the inclusion criteria.

### Data Extraction and Analysis

Twenty of the 55 studies that met the inclusion criteria reported results in a manner that allowed quantitative comparison to other studies and contained results that were not previously reported (i.e., in the 55 studies). Fire regime parameter values were systematically extracted and summarized from the subset of these studies that addressed a given parameter. Studies provided quantitative estimates in comparable ways for fire frequency, climate, and seasonality (table 1). Approaches for deriving information on fire severity and fire extent were inconsistent among studies and not comparable quantitatively. When a study included multiple, independent sites, each site-level value was included in the analysis. A database for each of the five fire regime parameters was created in Excel (Microsoft 365) and graphical and statistical analyses were performed for variables with sufficient quantitative data using R version 4.2.1 [29].

#### Fire Frequency

I extracted site-level mean, minimum, and maximum fire interval values (years), associated time period, elevation (m), and latitude and longitude (decimal degrees) from 20 studies comprising 108 independent sites (tables 1 and 2). Site elevation, latitude, and longitude were derived from maps and site descriptions when values were not explicitly provided. In cases lacking enough information to derive any of the site-specific values, I used study-level values provided in the publications. When only minimum and maximum elevation values were given in publications, I calculated the mid-point value and used it as the site elevation.

Mean fire interval (MFI) was the primary response variable for fire frequency analysis. MFI can be calculated and reported in multiple ways using different combinations of the same raw data (e.g., by time period, elevation band, number or percentage of trees affected, etc.). I did not double count results from studies that reported multiple MFI values derived from the same raw data. Likewise, when a study reported MFI values derived using subsets of fire dates based on different filtering criteria (e.g., a minimum number or percentage of trees scarred in a given year), I only included results from the most inclusive level. I did, however, include multiple MFI values from a given study if they were from independent sites and or independent time periods (i.e., different raw data).

All studies calculated composite MFI (i.e., dates of fire scars from all samples were combined into one crossdated time series), with 83% of site-level MFI values calculated without a filter (‘all-trees’, hereafter). In instances where a study failed to report MFI values for the all-trees category but reported MFI values derived using multiple filter levels, I chose the most inclusive level (least restrictive filter). For example, if a study reported MFI values derived from fire years recorded on at least 10% and 25% of all sampled trees, I included the MFI values based on the 10% filter.

Some fire history studies used the Weibull distribution to calculate measures of central tendency in fire interval data (usually the median is reported) instead of the normal distribution. The Weibull is a probability distribution and is appropriate for modeling the waiting time between events and it has been demonstrated to be a better fit for fire interval data than the normal distribution. Although the Weibull is likely a preferable probability distribution given the nature of fire interval data, too few studies reported Weibull median fire intervals to justify using it over the ubiquitously reported MFI as the response variable for the fire frequency meta-analysis. Moreover, the raw data that is analyzed at the study level (i.e., tree-level fire intervals) are different from the raw data I am using in this study (i.e., site-level means), and these latter data more closely match a normal distribution than tree-level interval data.

I performed graphical analyses to explore and describe patterns in the fire frequency data. I created a histogram of all MFI values extracted from the literature. I then plotted MFI value by corresponding site elevation and calculated Pearson’s correlation coefficient between MFI and elevation to assess the strength of the linear relationship between elevation and MFI.

Sites were grouped by ecoregion (Southern Rocky Mountains and New Mexico Mountains), and I created box plots to explore whether ecoregion was associated with variation in MFI.

#### Fire Severity

Information on fire severity reported within the fire history studies was not suitable for quantitative summary or comparison because methods used to infer severity, time periods analyzed, and metrics employed varied greatly among studies. Apart from low-severity fires recorded in fire scars, severity of fires that occurred prior to historical records is difficult to determine. Other syntheses of fire regime research report similarly large variation in methods and approach and associated difficulties of deriving comparative or summary information (e.g., [27]). Therefore, instead of extracting and summarizing numerical values on fire severity, descriptions of historical fire severity are summarized for each of the 12 papers that addressed this component of the fire regime.

#### Fire Extent

Describing the spatial distribution of wildfires within a given ecosystem, and whether and how the distribution varies across time and with environmental factors, is a key component to a full understanding of the fire regime. Information on fire extent can include, but is not limited to, the size of area burned by individual fires, the distribution of fire size classes, or the total area burned by all fires within a specified time period. However, similar to estimating severity of past fires, there is often a lack of strong evidence from which to estimate historical fire extent in ponderosa pine ecosystems. Important information is either lost over time, for example scarred trees die and decay, or fires fail to scar trees and therefore leave no record of their occurrence [27].

The papers evaluated in this fire regime synthesis that addressed fire extent did so in a general, less precise way, thereby avoiding potential biases and inconsistencies with attempting to quantify sizes of historical fires. Widespread fires or widespread fire years are defined by a minimum number of sites recording fire, and hence, the focus can be on assessing the ‘largest fire years’ and not the ‘largest fires’. Studies differed in their definitions of how many, or what percentage of, burned sites are needed to qualify a year as having widespread fires. I present the general relationships and consistent themes identified based on the findings of these studies.

#### Fire Seasonality

In fire history studies, fire seasonality data are reported as the percentage (or number) of fire scars by seasonal position within annual growth rings. Information on the percentage of early-season fire scars, time period analyzed, and study location was extracted from the studies that addressed the fire seasonality component of the fire regime (n = 8 studies, table 1). Early-season fire scars are those located in dormant and early-earlywood positions and are assumed to indicate fires that occurred in spring to early summer (April to June).

#### Fire-Climate Relationship

The fire-climate relationship was evaluated by selecting studies that performed superposed epoch analysis on the association between fire year and climatic indices for the fire year and each of the 4 years prior (n = 12 studies, table 1). Superposed epoch analysis (SEA) is a statistical method used to measure responses to an event and how a given variable relates prior to and during the event. The purpose of such an analysis is to determine whether patterns can be discerned in a time-series consisting of an event and putative explanatory variables. In fire history studies that implement SEA, fire year is the event, and reconstructed climatic indices are the explanatory variables [11].

Results were extracted from each SEA (n = 28 analyses) on the departure of a given climate index from the running mean for the fire year and for each of the 4 years preceding the fire year. I derived this information from SEA results figures that display index departures as positive or negative relative to the running average (zero line). The direction of departure for each index indicates the same relationship with precipitation; positive departures indicate above-average precipitation (wet conditions) and negative departures indicate below-average precipitation (dry conditions). I therefore recorded dry, wet, or normal for each of the 5 years analyzed within each SEA based on a given year’s departure from the zero line. Graphical analysis was performed to determine whether there was a distinction in the temporal pattern of precipitation conditions by plotting the percentage of results in each climate condition across the 5 years (fire year and the 4 preceding years) for all sites and as a comparison between the New Mexico Mountains and Southern Rocky Mountains ecoregions.

## Results

### Fire Frequency

Sample period length ranged from 57 years to 340 years with a mean of 201 years (SD = 69 years). The earliest fire scar date used in the fire frequency analyses was 1513—although older fire scar dates exist in the overall record—and the latest date was 1993. The last fire scar was recorded in the 17th century at 2 sites (1.8%), in the 18th century at 7 sites (6.5%), in the 19th century at 66 sites (61.1%), and in the 20th century at 33 sites (30.5%) (table A1). However, only 2 of the 33 sites that ended in the 20th century had fire scar records that extended beyond 1905 (1930 and 1993). This suggests that low-severity fires (i.e., that scarred trees) ended prior to 1906 at 98% of the sites.

Data extracted from the literature were insufficient to allow for a detailed time-series analysis on fire frequency (i.e., changes in frequency over time). At a coarse level, however, frequency varied little over time until the near cessation of all fires in the early 20th century. The mean MFI was 7.3 years (_n_ = 9) for sites where the fire record ended prior to 1800, and 9.4 years for sites where the record ended between 1800 and 1900 (_n_ = 93). Only six sites recorded the last fire after 1900, and of these six, only two sites had a fire recorded after 1905 (1930 and 1993). The mean MFI of these six sites was 10.7 years, but the inference from this data subset is limited given the small sample size and broad length of time (92 years) between the final fire scar dates (1901 to 1993).

Mean MFI of all sites (n = 108) was 9.3 years, with relatively low variability among sites (SE = 0.58 years). The range of MFI values among sites was only 37 years with a minimum of 2 years and maximum of 39 years (fig. 2). All but two sites had MFI values less than 30 years (a value generally used to differentiate a high frequency fire regime), and 94% of sites had MFI less than 20 years.

Taken together, these results indicate that fire frequency is consistent for these parts of New Mexico and has little to no relationship (i.e., variability) with elevation, geographic location, ecoregion, or time period (up to about 1905). This contrasts with fire frequency in ponderosa pine forests to the north in Colorado where both elevation and geographic location had strong relationships with fire frequency [27].

### Fire Severity

Over the past 6,000 years, the historical fire regime of New Mexico ponderosa pine forests can be generally described as having long periods of frequent, low-severity fires punctuated by shorter periods when higher severity fires were more common [9],[7]. High-severity fires were often associated with periods of extreme or extended drought conditions. Over the past ~700 years, these forests experienced frequent, low-severity fires, with some years experiencing higher-severity fire—as defined by a higher percentage of scarred trees [16],[26]. This period of frequent, low-severity fires generally ended by 1900 (e.g., [14],[3],[32]), with high-severity fires occurring after prolonged periods without fire during the 20th and 21st centuries (e.g., [8],[14]). The latter relationship is usually attributed to the effects of Euro-American settlement, especially associated livestock grazing, which reduced fine fuels that would otherwise have carried low-severity fires and resulted in longer fire-free periods than occurred historically (e.g., [3]). Long periods without fire can lead to increased stand densities and increased abundance and connectivity of fuels over time, which can ultimately result in large and severe fires. This relationship is pronounced during warm and drought periods that follow fire-free periods.

#### Past Centuries

Evidence from fire-scarred trees, tree remnants, and shrubfields:

 * A frequent and low-severity fire regime was documented over a 250-year period (1625–1871) in a ponderosa pine forest in the Jemez Mountains in north-central New Mexico. The period was followed by a 122-year fire-free interval which began in the same period as the introduction of livestock. The fire-free period ended in 1993 with a high-severity fire that led to vegetative type conversion from ponderosa pine forest to Gambel oak shrubland. The long fire-free period led to the accumulation of fuels, and these conditions coupled with high winds led to a high-severity fire that was anomalous at the site for at least the preceding 400 years [14].
 * Frequent and widespread, low-intensity surface fires characterized the fire regime of ponderosa pine forests in the Jemez Mountains of northwest New Mexico from the mid-1400’s to 1900. The fire-scar record shows an abrupt end to fire activity after 1899, but the spatial distribution of fires began declining by as early as the mid 1800’s. Because active fire suppression did not begin until the late 1910’s, the authors suggest that overgrazing by domestic livestock reduced fine fuels and thus prevented fire spread and excluded fire from these sites [3].
 * Fire frequency and putative fire severity seem to have changed in Capulin Volcano National Monument (northeastern New Mexico) between the time periods of 1600–1750 and 1751–1890, with shorter fire intervals (5.4 versus 19.1 years) and a lower percentage of trees scarred by fire during the former (14%) than the latter (55%) period. The percentage of fire-scarred trees was used as a surrogate for fire severity, and hence, a much higher percentage of trees scarred per fire during the latter period was interpreted as an increase in fire severity. The years 1770, 1815, and 1851 had the highest percentages of trees scarred per fire during the sampled time period, but severity and drought were not significantly correlated [16].
 * In ponderosa pine forests of the Santa Fe municipal watershed (north-central New Mexico), fire frequency was high and fire severity low throughout most of a 700-year sample period, although evidence of high-severity fires was identified during 24 individual fire years. Fire frequency and spatial extent were used to infer severity, where fire scars recorded over extensive areas were interpreted as high-severity fires when they occurred after extended fire-free periods. Synchrony between fires in ponderosa pine forests and higher elevation mixed conifer forests was considered evidence of mixed-severity fire and these fire years were characterized by more severe drought relative to years when fires burned only in ponderosa pine forests. The greatest single-year drought (1685) identified over the 700-year sample period was associated with the last large, high-severity fire that was mostly stand-replacing in the upper elevation spruce-dominated forest, and recorded as fire scars at 68% of the plots in the mixed conifer and ponderosa pine forests [26].
 * A fire regime dominated by high-frequency and low-severity fire was identified between 1700 and1880 in ponderosa pine forests of the Zuni Mountains in northwestern New Mexico. Evidence of fire activity ended around 1880, coinciding with Euro-American settlement and associated domestic livestock grazing. High intensity logging (e.g., clear-cutting) and the establishment of rail and road networks began in the early 20th century, leading to further reduction in wildfire occurrence, and associated increases in stand densities and the likelihood of high-severity fires [32].
 * The 1977 La Mesa fire in Bandelier National Monument burned at high severity on sites that had not recorded a fire for 37 to 117 years, while sites that burned at low severity had experienced a fire within the prior 1 to 17 years. The mean fire interval from 1786 to 1962 was 17.4 years, and sites with longer fire intervals were at higher risk for subsequent high-severity fire. When the fire interval was greater than 25 years, the subsequent fire killed nearly all mature trees. Where fire frequency was less than 25 years, 60% of mature trees survived subsequent fire [8].

#### Past Millennia

Evidence from paleo-debris flows and sediment records:

 * The two lines of evidence (fire scars and charcoal sediment) from two watersheds in northern New Mexico indicate a widespread, low-severity fire regime existed for centuries and then abruptly ended near the beginning of the 20th century. Fire scars recorded in the same year among trees broadly distributed in space were interpreted as a widespread, low-severity fire regime. At Chihuahueños Bog, nine widespread fires were recorded between 1624 and 1902, with a mean interval between widespread fire years of 34.8 years. The last recorded fires at the site occurred in 1857 and 1902. Bog sediment records indicate consistent charcoal deposition beginning 8,000 years ago and ending around 1900. The same pattern was evident at nearby Alamo Bog where low severity, widespread surface fires were recorded between 1624 and 1879 at an average frequency of 12.8 years, with 1879 being the last widespread fire year. A 9,000-year bog sediment sample shows consistent charcoal deposition throughout that ends abruptly by the late 1800’s [2].
 * Evidence from alluvial fan deposits suggests frequent low-severity fires and infrequent high-severity fires were both important aspects of the ponderosa pine fire regime over the past ~6,000 years in the Sacramento Mountains of south-central New Mexico. The changes in severity over time were thought to be influenced by broad-scale changes in climate over time, with severe fires being more common during warm and dry periods and during periods when multidecadal climate variability was high. High climatic variability produces multidecadal wet periods where surface fires are less common and stand densities increase. When these periods are followed by severe drought, high-severity fires and fire-related sediment deposition in alluvial fans are more likely. A mixed- and high-severity fire regime was evident in alluvial fan deposits from the middle Holocene (5800–4200 years BP). Evidence of high fire activity around 650 years ago corresponds to a period of widespread, severe drought in the Southwest. Little fire-related evidence from 500 to 100 years ago corresponds to the Little Ice Age. During the previous 500 years, a frequent low-severity fire regime dominated, but evidence from debris flows indicates that at least one large and severe fire occurred during this recent period [9],[7].

#### Contemporary Change

High-severity fires observed over the last several decades in ponderosa pine ecosystems in New Mexico were rare over the past 5 centuries, but perhaps not over the past 5 millennia [9]. The dominant fire regime characterized by frequent, low-severity fires has largely ceased [2],[32]. Higher-severity crown and stand-replacing fires have occurred in recent times [8],[17],[33]. Both the area burned at high severity and the percentage of high-severity fires increased in New Mexico ponderosa pine forests from 1984 to 2015 [35].

### Fire Extent

Two consistent general relationships emerge from the studies that addressed fire extent—variability in fire extent correlated with climate over time and changes associated with Euro-American presence and activity. None of the fire history studies examined for this synthesis attempted to provide quantitative estimates of historical fire sizes or size class distribution, or how those metrics have changed over time. Several studies did define, identify, and quantify widespread fire years, and they discussed them within the context of fire-climate relationships, fire seasonality, and human history. Overall, regional fire-scar chronologies demonstrate that over the previous 5 centuries, widespread fire was a common, recurring event linked to dry years that were often preceded by wet years and conducive to the open stand structure and continuous surface vegetation typical of many ponderosa pine sites throughout New Mexico [1]. However, in the southwestern Jemez Mountains, fire patterns shifted as indigenous population densities changed [31],[24],[38]. Before the Hemish population collapsed (approximately 1500–1650), fires were small and frequent—with no fires scarring more than 25% of trees. However, after 1650, widespread, climate-synchronized surface fires (i.e., scarring >25% of trees) became a regular occurrence [31]. These chronologies also consistently indicate a decline in frequency and near cessation of widespread fire years coincident with Euro-American settlement during the mid to late 19th century. This decline is attributed primarily to domestic livestock grazing reducing the abundance and continuity of fine fuels, and secondarily to active fire suppression and exclusion during the early to mid-20th century [3].

Prolonged periods of drought, or periods of severe drought, are associated with widespread fire years, which are defined by a minimum number of sites recording fire, and not the average size of individual fires. For example, when widespread fire years were defined as those in which >30% of study sites across a broad region of the Southwest (New Mexico and Arizona) recorded a fire (i.e., fire scar) [36], 20 widespread fire years were identified between 1700 and 1900, and no widespread fire years since 1900. Climatic variability at a regional spatial scale and annual temporal scale was thought to be the leading causal factor for the temporal and spatial pattern of widespread fire years because no other factor could influence fire occurrence over such a large area and broad time period. The largest fire years (percentage of sites recording a fire) were very dry years and were preceded by exceptionally wet years 2 and 3 years prior. For example, 1748 had the largest percentage of sites recording a fire (65%), and it was also one of the driest years during the sampled time period [36].

Widespread fire years were especially pronounced during dry years that followed 1 to 3 years of wetter-than-average conditions [1]. The hypothesized relationship between climate, vegetation dynamics, and fire extent is that wet conditions drive the growth and accumulation of vegetation, resulting in continuous receptive surface fuels that burn widely during subsequent dry years [1]. In the Frijoles watershed, widespread fires (fire scars recorded throughout the watershed in the same year) occurred in 8 years over a 158-year period (1684–1842) [3]. Despite the strong relationship between seasonal drought and widespread fires within years, persistent, multi-year drought does not lead to increased widespread fire. Fires commonly occur in the first and second year of multi-year droughts, rather than years into a multi-year drought indicating that short duration droughts are an important driver of fires in New Mexico ponderosa pine forests. Prolonged droughts may limit large fires because of fuel limitations; once fires burn, precipitation is needed to grow fine fuels before the next fire can occur [25].

Estimates of the time between widespread fires are similar among New Mexico ponderosa pine study sites. From 1624 to 1902, the time between widespread fires ranged from 10 to 61 years at a study site in the Jemez Mountains where widespread fires were defined as years with at least 10% of previously scarred sample trees recording a fire scar, and with at least two trees scarred [2]. At another site, approximately 20 km away, the time between widespread fires ranged from 4 to 27 years between 1624 and 1879. In Bandelier National Monument, a landscape consisting of isolated mesas populated by open ponderosa pine stands, the time between widespread fires ranged from 12 to 25 years between 1786 and 1900 [8].

Before the Pueblo Revolt in 1680, land use by American Indians in the Jemez Mountains both reduced the frequency of widespread fires and increased the frequency of fire ignitions, resulting in numerous small-extent fires [38]. American Indian populations had declined by 87% in this region a century following European contact, and this population change is linked to changes in the frequency of widespread fires [24]. Between 1640 and 1900, the frequency of widespread fires increased relative to the preceding period when American Indians controlled widespread fire occurrence through their land use practices [24].

By the 1880s millions of sheep and cattle were grazing throughout the Southwest, and during the same period evidence of low-severity and broadly spreading fire mostly ends [1]. This relationship is also evident at finer spatial scales. For example, widespread fires abruptly ended after 1851 in the Frijoles watershed [3], and the last widespread fire recorded around Alamo Bog in the Jemez Mountains occurred in 1879 [2]. In the Jemez Mountains, evidence of widespread fire ends earlier in the valleys where livestock were initially close to Euro-American settlements, and later in the more remote uplands where livestock grazing began later [1]. In contrast, evidence for the persistence of a natural fire regime, including widespread fire, continued in locations where Euro-American settlement and associated livestock did not occur, such as the extensive lava flows of El Malpais National Monument and the remote Animas Mountains [1].

By the early to mid-20th century, evidence of widespread fire, and indeed nearly all wildfire, abruptly ends in most accessible sites with the advent of fire exclusion management policies and active fire suppression. For example, at two intensively studied bog sites with charcoal records dating back 9,000 years, an abrupt end to fire after 1900 is evident, demonstrating that the recent century-long period of nearly absent fire is unique at millennial time scales [2].

### Fire Seasonality

The fire scar record indicates that most historical fires in ponderosa pine ecosystems in New Mexico were early-season fires (spring to early summer), occurring prior to the onset of monsoon rains (table 3). Based on the intra-annual tree ring position of fire scars, most fire scars were located in the dormant and early-earlywood positions, which are attributed to fires occurring between April and June. All eight of the fire history studies that provided results of fire scar seasonality showed a higher proportion of early-season fire scars (min 51%, max 97%, mean 69%) relative to fire scars from mid-summer or fall (table 3). The studies collectively sampled trees with fire scars spanning over 6 centuries, suggesting a persistent relationship between pre-monsoonal dry conditions and early season lightning ignitions.

Early-season fires are most strongly associated with cool-season drought, but cool-season drought is also related to late season fires (late summer to fall), just not as strongly [25]. Monsoon season drought is strongly associated with late-season fires, and to a lesser extent, with mid-season fires [25]. Widespread late-season fires appear to be related to drought in both cool and monsoon seasons [25], or these may be related to human-caused ignitions [22].

Allen (1989) [3] notes that the climate regime of summer thunderstorms preceded by spring drought is believed to have persisted in the American Southwest for about the past 8,000 years. Early-season fires that occur in a year with monsoon season drought have the potential to spread and burn into mid and late summer, otherwise they are usually extinguished by monsoonal rains.

### Fire-Climate Relationship

All 28 Superposed Epoch Analyses showed drier-than-average conditions during the fire year (y-0) and wetter-than-average conditions in each of the 4 years preceding the fire year (fig. 4). The strength of the latter relationship declined with time before fire. In the 2 years immediately preceding the fire year, 93% of results indicated wetter-than-average conditions, whereas 4 years before the fire year, 54% of results indicated wetter-than-average conditions. The pattern of wetter-than-average conditions in the immediate years preceding a fire year is indicative of a fuel-limited system, whereby biomass production that results from increased moisture availability is desiccated and burned in subsequent drier-than-average fire years [2].

## Conclusion

The information derived from the studies that comprise this fire regime synthesis consistently portray a fire regime that for at least the last 6 centuries, was characterized by frequent (< 20-year interval), low-severity fires that spread among broadly spaced mature trees by continuous fine surface fuels. Fires occurred most often in spring, prior to the onset of the summer monsoon, and during drier-than-average years that were typically preceded by 1 to 2 years of wetter-than-average conditions. Widespread fire years—those in which a greater proportion of trees were scarred over larger areas—were associated with drought conditions.

A longer fire record (~9,000 years) obtained from charcoal sediments, also indicates a consistent presence of fire on the landscape. This record lacks the temporal precision of the fire-scar record, but corroborates observations that fire was consistently present on the landscape and burned in greater magnitudes spatially and intensively during periods of extended or severe drought.

Also consistent among the studies and supported by century- and millennial-scale evidence is the abrupt change in fire occurrence across broad regions. By the mid-17th century, ongoing declines in American Indian populations are associated with an increase in the frequency of widespread fires. Prior to this period, small-extent fires were more frequent. The next change to the fire regime occurred around the mid-19th century (specific timing dependent on location) and is linked primarily to the introduction of livestock by Euro-American settlers. The hypothesized relationship is that as livestock numbers grew, the abundance and continuity of fine surface fuels were decreased by grazing to the extent that the frequency of low-intensity and surface-spreading fires was reduced. The second effect came later, during the early to mid-20th century when active and effective fire suppression began. During this period a near-complete cessation of fire activity is observed in the fire-scar and charcoal-sediment records. The hypothesized effect is that the absence of fire from a system where fire was a frequent and integral component of ecosystem dynamics led to increased stand density beyond levels thought to have occurred for centuries. Increased stand density coupled with climatic fluctuations resulting in extended dry periods led to the high-severity and spatially extensive burns witnessed in the 21st century. These recent fires, then, are uncharacteristic to what has been documented for at least the past 500 years.
